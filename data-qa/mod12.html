Module 12: Bacterial Genetics — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is a chromosome?

    • A closed loop of double-stranded DNA (in bacteria) that stores the genetic information of the cell.
    • It is very long but only ~2 nm wide, so it is supercoiled to fit inside the cell (picture an old phone cord coiling on itself).

  2. How are prokaryotic and eukaryotic chromosomes similar and different?

    • Similar: both are made of double-stranded DNA with the same basic structure.
    • Prokaryotic: circular (a closed loop — like two braided rubber bands); usually a single chromosome.
    • Eukaryotic: linear (has ends — like two braided shoelaces); multiple chromosomes (humans have 46).

  3. What is a gene?

    • A region of a chromosome that encodes the primary structure (the amino-acid sequence) of a specific protein — a “recipe” for one protein.
    • E. coli has ~5,000 genes.

  4. What is a genotype?

    • The genes and other nucleotide sequences present in the genome — i.e., what the DNA letters actually say.

  5. What is a phenotype and how do phenotypes relate to genotypes?

    • The physical, behavioral, and metabolic properties of an organism that result from the genotype.
    • Genotype determines phenotype. Example (E. coli): rod shape, Gram-negative cell wall, facultative anaerobe — all phenotypes made possible by its genes.

  6. What did Craig Venter do?

    • Helped sequence the human genome (developed a fast DNA-sequencing method).
    • Created the first artificial genome: removed and sequenced the chromosome of Mycoplasma genitalium (only 521 genes), synthesized an artificial copy, and injected it into a cell that had lost its chromosome — the cell “rebooted” and ran on it.
    • Called “artificial life” by some; Venter disagrees, since he relied on the existing cell’s membrane, ribosomes, etc.

  7. What happened during Frederick Griffith’s experiment?

      1. Used two strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae: smooth (S) — has a capsule, pathogenic; rough (R) — no capsule, harmless.
    • Live S → mouse died. Live R → mouse lived. Heat-killed S → mouse lived.
    • Heat-killed S + live R → mouse died, and its body was full of living S cells.
    • Conclusion: some “genetic material” leaked from the dead S cells and was taken up by the R cells, transforming them into pathogenic S cells — but what that material was remained unknown.

  8. How did Avery’s experiment demonstrate that DNA is the genetic material?

    • (1944; Avery–MacLeod–McCarty) Repeated Griffith’s heat-killed S + live R mix, in two versions:
    • + Protease (destroys protein) → mouse still died → protein is not the transforming material.
    • + DNase (destroys DNA) → mouse lived → destroying DNA blocked transformation.
    • Therefore DNA is the genetic material.

  9. Who were Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase? (not in recorded lecture) *

    • Sourced from instructor slides + textbook (Ch. 10) — not in the video.
      1. Their experiments confirmed that DNA (not protein) is the genetic material.
    • Used the T2 bacteriophage infecting E. coli: labeled phage protein with radioactive sulfur (³⁵S) in one batch and DNA with radioactive phosphorus (³²P) in another.
    • After infection, blending, and centrifuging, the ³²P (DNA) was inside the bacterial cells, while the ³⁵S (protein) stayed outside → the phage injects DNA, which directs production of new phages.

  10. Which two scientists published the first paper describing the structure of DNA?

    • James Watson and Francis Crick — a one-page paper in Nature with no experimental data; they built molecular models of the four nucleotides until one fit the known data.
    • Nobel Prize in 1962 (shared with Maurice Wilkins, Franklin’s boss).

  11. What was Rosalind Franklin’s contribution to the structure of DNA?

    • Pioneered X-ray crystallography: crystallized DNA, bombarded it with X-rays, and produced Photo 51, which showed that DNA is a double helix (her notes: phosphates on the outside).
    • Watson saw Photo 51 without her permission; it guided their model.
    • She died at 38 (likely from X-ray exposure) and the Nobel Prize is not awarded posthumously.

  12. What is the structure of DNA?

    • A double-stranded polymer of nucleotides twisted into a double helix.
    • Sugar-phosphate backbone (covalent bonds) on the outside; complementary base pairs (A–T, G–C) on the inside, held by hydrogen bonds — like a twisted ladder.
    • The two strands are antiparallel (one runs 5’→3’, the other 3’→5’).
    • Constant width of ~2 nm, but can be extremely long.

  13. What is the structure of RNA?

    • Typically single-stranded; sugar is ribose (not deoxyribose); bases A, G, C, and U (uracil) — no thymine.

  14. What are nucleotides?

    • The monomers of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA); linked by dehydration synthesis.
    • Three parts: a phosphate group (negative charge), a 5-carbon (pentose) sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a nitrogenous base — the base is what makes each nucleotide unique.

  15. Which nucleotides are used in DNA?

    • Adenine (A), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C), Thymine (T).

  16. Which nucleotides are used in RNA?

    • Adenine (A), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C), Uracil (U). Uracil replaces thymine and still pairs with A.

  17. What is complementary base pairing and how does it occur?

    • Specific bases hydrogen-bond with each other across the two strands: A pairs with T (U in RNA) and G pairs with C.
    • A–T = 2 hydrogen bonds; G–C = 3 hydrogen bonds (slides).
    • Consequence: in dsDNA, %A = %T and %G = %C. (Slide challenge: 25% A → 25% T, 25% G, 25% C; 34% G → 34% C, 16% A, 16% T.)

  18. What is meant by 5’ and 3’ (as it relates to nucleic acid structure)?

    • Refers to the numbered carbons of the sugar. The 5’ end starts with a phosphate attached to the 5th carbon; the 3’ end ends with an –OH on the 3rd carbon.
    • The two DNA strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel).
    • “Golden rule” (slides): nucleic acids can only be synthesized 5’ → 3’.

  19. What roles do hydrogen bonds play in DNA structure?

    • Hold the two strands together down the middle (between complementary bases).
    • Repetitive hydrogen bonding also twists the molecule into its helical shape.
    • Because H-bonds are weak (unlike the covalent backbone), the strands can be separated for replication.

  20. What is the purpose of DNA replication?

    • To duplicate (copy) the chromosome so each daughter cell receives a copy during binary fission.
    • It is semi-conservative: each new double helix has one old (parent) strand + one new (daughter) strand.
    • Takes ~30 minutes under ideal conditions — most of a bacterium’s generation time.

  21. What do gyrase, helicase, primase, polymerase, and ligase do in replication?

    • Gyrase — uncoils the supercoiled DNA.
    • Helicase — unzips and unwinds the double helix, forming the replication fork.
    • Primase — lays down a short RNA primer that gives polymerase a starting point.
    • DNA polymerase — adds complementary nucleotides (5’→3’) to build the new strand; replaces primers with DNA; proofreads.
    • Ligase — glues (joins) Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand.

  22. What is a replication fork?

    • The Y-shaped region where helicase has separated the two DNA strands — two “prongs” like a fork in the road.

  23. What is an origin of replication?

    • The specific site on a bacterial chromosome (or plasmid) where helicases bind and replication begins.
    • Two helicases leave the origin in opposite directions, creating two replication forks that travel around the loop until they meet — giving two identical copies.

  24. How does synthesis differ between the leading and lagging daughter strands?

    • Because polymerase/primase can only build 5’ → 3’:
    • Leading strand — built continuously in one piece, toward the fork, following the helicase.
    • Lagging strand — built away from the fork in short pieces (Okazaki fragments), each needing its own primer; fragments are joined by ligase, so it “lags” behind.

  25. How does the antibiotic ciprofloxacin (Cipro) work?

    • Inhibits bacterial DNA gyrase → DNA can’t be uncoiled → can’t be replicated → bacteria can’t grow.
    • Broad-spectrum; doesn’t harm human cells because our version of the enzyme is different.

  26. What is the name given to the 5’ → 3’ DNA strand? (not in recorded lecture) *

    • Sourced from instructor slides. The coding strand — it has the same sequence and orientation as the mRNA (with T instead of U).

  27. What is the name given to the 3’ → 5’ DNA strand? (not in recorded lecture) *

    • Sourced from instructor slides. The non-coding (template) strand — it is used as the template to synthesize mRNA during transcription.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Genome — The complete set of an organism’s genetic material (its DNA — chromosome plus any plasmids). Not explicitly defined in the lecture; textbook-level definition.
  • Semi-conservative model — See Q20 — each new DNA helix = one parent strand + one daughter strand.
  • Sugar-phosphate bonds — Covalent bonds that form the strong backbone of each strand (slides); the backbone carries an overall negative charge.
  • Anti-parallel / 5’→3’ / 3’→5’ — See Q12 and Q18.
  • Leading / Lagging daughter strand — See Q24.
  • Okazaki fragments — Short DNA pieces of the lagging strand, joined by ligase.
