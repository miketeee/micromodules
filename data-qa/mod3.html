Study Guide Questions

  1. What are the primary shapes of bacterial cells? Provide examples of each.
    •  Coccus (round) — e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae
    •  Bacillus (rod-shaped) — e.g., Bacillus anthracis
    •  Vibrio (curved rod) / Spirillum (loose spiral) — e.g., Helicobacter pylori
    •  Spirochete (tight, rigid spiral) — e.g., Treponema pallidum (syphilis)

  2. What are the primary arrangements of bacterial cells? Provide examples.
    •  Diplo- (pairs) — e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae (diplococcus)
    •  Strepto- (chains) — e.g., Streptococcus pyogenes
    •  Staphylo- (clusters) — e.g., Staphylococcus aureus
    •  These prefixes can attach to either cocci or bacilli (e.g., diplobacillus, streptobacillus — Bacillus anthracis, Bacillus cereus).

  3. What are flagella made of? What do they do? How do they contribute to disease?
    •  Made of protein, forming a complex rotary motor structure powered by a proton motive force (H+ flow).
    •  Used for motility — bacterial flagella rotate (unlike eukaryotic flagella, which whip).
    •  Contribute to disease by letting pathogens move toward and invade host tissue (e.g., Helicobacter pylori swimming through stomach mucus to reach the stomach lining).

  4. What are the four flagellar arrangements? Name and define each.
    •  Monotrichous — one flagellum at one end.
    •  Amphitrichous — one flagellum at each end.
    •  Lophotrichous — a tuft of flagella at one end.
    •  Peritrichous — flagella distributed all around the cell (e.g., Salmonella).

  5. What are fimbriae made of? What do they do? How do they contribute to disease?
    •  Made of protein; short, hair-like filaments tipped with a sticky protein called an adhesin.
    •  Used for adherence (attachment) to surfaces.
    •  Contribute to disease as the first step of infection — allowing bacteria to attach to host tissue (e.g., E. coli fimbriae).

  6. What are pili made of? What do they do? How do they contribute to disease?
    •  Made of protein; the conjugation (sex) pilus is a longer tube than fimbriae.
    •  Used to exchange genetic material (including plasmids) between bacteria in a process called conjugation.
    •  Contribute to disease by spreading antibiotic-resistance genes between bacterial cells.

  7. What are capsules made of? What do they do? How do they contribute to disease?
    •  Made primarily of carbohydrate (sticky sugar material); also called the glycocalyx.
    •  Used for adherence and forming biofilms.
    •  Contribute to disease by helping bacteria stick to surfaces/tissue and resist removal, e.g., Streptococcus mutans forms a biofilm on teeth, causing cavities.

  8. What is a biofilm, and what structures allow bacteria to form them?
    •  A thin layer of bacteria adhered to a surface ("bio" = life, "film" = thin layer).
    •  Capsules (and fimbriae) let bacteria adhere to surfaces and each other to build a biofilm (e.g., S. mutans plaque on teeth).

  9. What is a cell wall? Why is the cell wall important?
    •  A rigid covering surrounding the cell membrane.
    •  Provides structural support and protects against excessive water uptake/osmotic pressure that would otherwise burst the cell.

  10. What is peptidoglycan? What two subunit substances make up peptidoglycan?
    •  The main substance of the bacterial cell wall.
    •  A polymer of two alternating sugars, NAG (N-acetylglucosamine) and NAM (N-acetylmuramic acid), cross-linked by short amino acid chains.

  11. Why are the amino acid cross-links in peptidoglycan important to bacteria?
    •  They give the cell wall its rigidity and strength, allowing it to resist osmotic pressure and mechanical stress that would otherwise tear the cell apart.

  12. What is the structure of the Gram-positive cell wall (G+ cell envelope)?
    •  A thick layer of peptidoglycan studded with teichoic acid and lipoteichoic acid, around a plasma membrane. No outer membrane. Stains purple.

  13. What is the structure of the Gram-negative cell wall (G- cell envelope)?
    •  A thin layer of peptidoglycan, plasma membrane beneath it, and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and membrane proteins. Stains pink.

  14. What is lysozyme, and how does it work?
    •  A digestive enzyme found in tears, saliva, and phagocytic cells.
    •  Catalyzes hydrolysis of peptidoglycan, breaking down the cell wall and causing the bacterial cell to lyse.

  15. What is penicillin, and how does it work?
    •  A beta-lactam antibiotic.
    •  Inhibits the dehydration synthesis of amino acid cross-links during peptidoglycan synthesis, weakening the new cell wall and causing lysis.

  16. What term is used to describe bacteria with waxy substances in their cell walls?
    •  Acid-Fast bacteria.

  17. What substance makes waxy cell walls, and why is it clinically relevant?
    •  Mycolic acid. It is very waxy, so it: confers resistance to phagocytosis, repels many antibiotics (harder to treat infections), and repels standard stains (requiring a special Acid-Fast stain). Example: Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

  18. Are bacteria with waxy cell walls more similar to Gram-positive or Gram-negative cells?
    •  More similar to Gram-positive: they have a peptidoglycan layer and no outer membrane, but mycolic acid is added, giving the waxy, stain-resistant property.

  19. What genus of bacteria lack cell walls but can still cause disease?
    •  Mycoplasma (e.g., Mycoplasma pneumoniae, causing "walking pneumonia"). They use sterols for membrane stability and are pleiomorphic since they have no rigid wall.

Prokaryotic Cell Structures (structure/function, from slides): - Cell membrane (plasma membrane) — encloses the cytoplasm; site of transport. - Cytoplasm — gelatinous internal fluid where biochemical reactions occur. - Inclusions — storage granules for excess nutrients (e.g., glycogen). - Nucleoid — region containing the circular bacterial chromosome. - Plasmid — small loop of DNA carrying "accessory" genes; can be exchanged via conjugation. - Ribosomes — translate mRNA into protein; bacterial ribosomes are 70S (vs. 80S in eukaryotes) — a target for antibiotics like tetracycline.

Scientist Spotlight - Robert Koch — see Module 1 (proved the Germ Theory of Disease). - Hans Christian Gram — developed the Gram Stain differential staining technique.
