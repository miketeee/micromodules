Module 18: Principles of Disease — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is pathology? Etiology? Pathogenesis?

    • Pathology = the study of disease (pathos = suffering, logos = science). It has two subdisciplines:
    • Etiology = the study of the cause of a disease. Ex: the cause of syphilis is Treponema pallidum. Non-infectious diseases (diabetes, cystic fibrosis) have etiologies too.
    • Pathogenesis = the manner in which a disease develops — transmission, how it affects the body, signs and symptoms, how long it lasts.

  2. What is disease? Infection? How do they differ?

    • Disease = a change in the body away from a state of health (away from homeostasis). Ex: AIDS.
    • Infection = the growth of microorganisms in/on the body. Ex: HIV — infection with HIV can lead to the disease AIDS, so the two terms are not interchangeable.
    • Not every disease is infectious (diabetes, cystic fibrosis). Infectious disease is the leading cause of death in the world but the 4th in the US.

  3. What are three types of symbiosis? How do they differ from each other?

    • Symbiosis = the living together of two different organisms. The human microbiome can exceed 1,000 species.
    • Mutualism (+/+): microbe and host both benefit. Ex: E. coli in the colon (makes most of our vitamin K, inhibits Salmonella).
    • Commensalism (+/0): microbe benefits, host is unaffected. Ex: Candida albicans on the skin.
    • Parasitism (+/−): microbe benefits, host is harmed (may die). Ex: Vibrio cholerae → cholera.

  4. What are resident microbiota (normal flora)?

    • The mutualistic and commensal microorganisms living in/on the body at all times.
    • Three big players: Lactobacillus acidophilus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Escherichia coli.
    • Slides: we carry ~30 trillion microbial cells (roughly 1:1 with our own cells); residents include bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoa, and viruses.

  5. How does the diversity of resident microbiota influence human health?

    • The more diverse the microbiome, the healthier you are likely to be — more species of normal flora means less room for pathogens (more microbial antagonism).
    • Lecture: a family’s last child usually has the most diverse microbiome (parents are less overprotective and exposure is greater).
    • Slides: loss of balance (dysbiosis) is linked to many conditions (allergies, asthma, obesity, IBD, and more).

  6. What is microbial antagonism? What are 3 common examples in humans?

    • Microbial antagonism = normal flora inhibiting the growth of other microbes, preventing infection by pathogens.
    • Outcompete: S. epidermidis covers the skin, leaving no space or nutrients for S. aureus.
    • Change the environment: Lactobacillus acidophilus makes acid, dropping vaginal pH to ~4–5 → Candida albicans can’t grow (antibiotics that kill Lactobacillus → yeast infection).
    • Secrete bacteriocins: E. coli releases bacteriocins that inhibit Salmonella and Shigella in the intestines.

  7. How are normal flora acquired (3 examples)?

    • The fetus starts sterile in the amniotic sac.
    • Lactobacillus acidophilus → vaginal delivery (mother’s Lactobacillus population surges just before birth).
    • Staphylococcus epidermidis → nursing and handling (skin-to-skin contact). S. aureus comes along too → baby acne until S. epidermidis outcompetes it.
    • E. coli → contaminated food and objects put in the mouth. Newborns lack it, so they get a vitamin K injection.
    • Slides: breast milk is a second wave (probiotics + prebiotics); the microbiome nears stability around age 2.

  8. What are opportunistic pathogens? Examples?

    • Microbes that are harmless in their normal bodily location but cause disease when introduced into other locations (or when host defenses are compromised — slides).
    • E. coli: harmless in the colon → UTI or wound infection elsewhere.
    • S. aureus: commensal on unbroken skin → wound infection through burns/cuts/surgery; also UTIs.
    • The two main causes of UTIs: E. coli (Gram-negative UTIs) and S. aureus (Gram-positive UTIs). Females are at higher risk (short distance anus → urethra, short urethra) — wipe front to back.
    • Contrast: true pathogens cause disease in healthy people with working immune systems (slides).

  9. What are Koch’s postulates? What are some limitations to Koch’s postulates?

    • Robert Koch’s procedure for finding the etiological cause of an infectious disease:
      1. Start with a diseased organism and isolate its microbes.
      1. Grow and identify each in pure culture (drove pure culturing and differential stains — Gram, endospore, capsule).
      1. Inject each pure culture into a healthy organism until one recreates the disease.
      1. Re-isolate that microbe, grow it in pure culture, and re-identify it.
    • Limitations: some diseases have multiple causes (sinusitis, UTI); some microbes can’t be pure-cultured; ethical concerns for human-only pathogens (Koch’s postulates have never been completed for HIV → AIDS). Slides also list human-specific pathogens and pathogens that cause different disease states.
    • Workaround: Barry Marshall (with Robin Warren) drank H. pylori and gave himself ulcers.

  10. What are symptoms? Signs? Examples?

    • Sign = an objective change that an observer can see or measure: fever, blood pressure, spots/rash.
    • Symptom = a subjective change felt by the patient: pain, tiredness, achiness, malaise, headache. (“Rate your pain 1–10.”)

  11. What is a communicable disease? Contagious? Non-communicable?

    • Communicable = can be spread from one host to another. Ex: flu.
    • Contagious = easily spread from one host to another. Ex: measles (slides: pox diseases, influenza, COVID, TB, MRSA). All contagious diseases are communicable, but not all communicable diseases are contagious.
    • Non-communicable = not transmitted between hosts. All non-infectious diseases (diabetes, cancer, heart disease) are non-communicable; some infectious ones are too — tetanus (slides add Lyme disease).

  12. How does incidence differ from prevalence?

    • Incidence = new cases during a time period (indicator of spread). Ex: US AIDS incidence 2014 ≈ 37,600.
    • Prevalence = all cases (new + existing) at a given time (indicator of burden). Ex: US AIDS prevalence 2014 ≈ 1.1 million.
    • Marble jar: incidence = marbles coming in; deaths and cures take marbles out; prevalence = marbles in the jar.

  13. How do sporadic, endemic, epidemic, and pandemics differ from each other?

    • Sporadic: occurs occasionally (outbreaks). Ex: cholera after flooding.
    • Endemic: constantly present in a population. Ex: common cold.
    • Epidemic: acquired by many hosts in a given area in a short time. Ex: a flu epidemic.
    • Pandemic: a worldwide epidemic. Ex: 1918 Spanish flu, AIDS.

  14. What is herd immunity? Why is it important?

    • Immunity in most of a population, bolstered by vaccination.
    • When most people are immune, the pathogen can’t spread, so even unvaccinated people are protected. Falling vaccination rates → rising measles incidence.

  15. How do acute, chronic, and latent infections differ from each other?

    • Acute: symptoms develop rapidly but last a short time. Ex: food poisoning.
    • Chronic: develops slowly, continues or recurs for long periods. Ex: tuberculosis (alveoli scar over time).
    • Latent: the pathogen stays in the host for long periods without producing disease, then can reactivate. Ex: herpes fever blisters (provirus).

  16. How do local and systemic infections differ?

    • Local: pathogens limited to a small area. Ex: abscess (boil, pimple, abscessed tooth); slides add fungal skin infections and warts.
    • Systemic: infection throughout the body — the microbe or its toxins spread, usually by blood or lymph. Ex: measles (spots everywhere); slides add chickenpox, typhoid fever, syphilis.

  17. What are subclinical (asymptomatic) infections?

    • An infection that does not cause a noticeable illness — infected, but no signs or symptoms. Ex: hepatitis A.

  18. What are carriers [of disease]?

    • Not covered in the lecture or slides — from textbook Ch. 16:
    • A carrier can transmit a pathogen without showing symptoms.
    • Passive carrier: contaminated but not infected; spreads it mechanically (e.g., a health-care worker who doesn’t wash hands).
    • Active carrier: infected and can transmit (during incubation or convalescence). Asymptomatic carriers never show signs — ex: Typhoid Mary (Mary Mallon, Salmonella typhi).

  19. What is bacteremia? Toxemia? Sepsis?

    • Bacteremia = bacteria in the blood (blood should be sterile).
    • Toxemia = toxins in the blood (the bacteria may stay in one spot).
    • Sepsis = an acute, extreme (hypersensitive) immune reaction to pathogens or toxins in the blood/tissues → can cause septic shock (blood pressure drop) or very high fever.
    • Slides add: septicemia = pathogens multiplying in blood (“blood poisoning”); viremia = viruses in the blood.

  20. What are primary and secondary infections?

    • Primary: an acute infection that causes the initial illness. Ex: sinusitis.
    • Secondary: caused by an opportunistic microbe after the primary infection weakens host defenses. Ex: sinus drainage carries S. pneumoniae from the upper to the lower respiratory tract → pneumonia.

  21. What are predisposing factors? Examples?

    • Anything that makes the body more susceptible to disease or alters its course.
    • Sex/gender: females → more UTIs. Age: elderly → pneumonia.
    • Slides add: climate, surgery/transplants, vaccination status, chemotherapy/immunosuppressive drugs, genetic defects, AIDS, cancer, diabetes, other infections, poor hygiene/nutrition, alcohol/drugs, smoking, pregnancy, physical and mental stress.

  22. What are the five stages of infectious disease? What is happening during each?

    • Like a growth curve inside the body (number of microbes vs time):
      1. Incubation period: microbes slowly increasing; no signs or symptoms.
      1. Prodromal period: numbers rising; mild signs/symptoms (“I’m coming down with something”).
      1. Period of illness: exponential growth; most severe signs and symptoms (fever, vomiting, mucus). Death may occur at the peak.
      1. Period of decline: immune system or antibiotics reduce microbe numbers; signs and symptoms subside.
      1. Period of convalescence: no signs or symptoms, but microbes aren’t gone yet → finish the whole antibiotic course, or the most resistant survivors start the next infection.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Pathogen — A disease-causing microorganism. Slides: pathogenicity = ability to overcome host defenses and cause disease; virulence factor = anything that confers pathogenicity.
  • Infectious vs non-infectious disease — Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens; non-infectious ones are not (genetics, aging, diet, organ malfunction).
  • Microbiome — The collective microbial community and its “theatre of activity” (slides); ours carries ~232 million genes vs ~22,000 human genes.
  • Probiotics / prebiotics / fecal transplants — Slides: live beneficial microbes (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) / nutrients that feed them (fiber, fructans) / transfer of healthy feces — used for recurring C. difficile.
  • Bacteriocins — Chemicals secreted by bacteria that inhibit other bacteria (E. coli vs Salmonella and Shigella).
