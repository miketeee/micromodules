Module 10: Eukaryotes — Fungi — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What are some distinguishing characteristics of fungi?
    •  Unicellular (yeast) or multicellular (mold); heterotrophic (decomposers); non-motile; cell wall made of chitin; some cause disease (mycosis).

  2. How do yeasts differ from molds?
    •  Yeasts: unicellular, facultative anaerobes, reproduce asexually by binary fission or budding.
    •  Molds: multicellular, aerobic, reproduce both asexually and sexually by means of spores.

  3. What is the most common process yeasts use for reproduction? Describe the process and microscopic appearance.
    •  Budding. The cell doesn't split in half — it grows a small extension ("mini me") off to the side, copies its chromosomes into that bud, which eventually breaks off to form a new cell.
    •  Microscopically: a parent cell with one or more small buds protruding, sometimes across multiple generations; a broken-off bud leaves a bud scar in the chitin cell wall.

  4. Why is Saccharomyces cerevisiae important?
    •  It's baker's/brewer's yeast — its name literally means "the fungus that turns sugar into beer."
    •  Ferments sugar to produce CO2 (leavened bread) and ethanol (beer, wine).

  5. What are hyphae?
    •  Filaments of fungal cells making up a mold; one filament is a hypha, many are hyphae.

  6. What are spores?
    •  Small reproductive structures molds use to start new growth — a spore landing on a food source begins mitosis and grows into a network of hyphae.

  7. How do mold sporangiospores differ from conidiospores?
    •  Sporangiospores — asexual spores enclosed on a sporangiophore.
    •  Conidiospores — asexual spores exposed on the outside of a conidiophore.

  8. What type of spores do Aspergillus and Penicillium produce?
    •  Conidiospores — they belong to the fungal group Ascomycota, which uses asexual spores called conidia (unlike sporangiospore-producing molds such as Rhizopus, common bread mold).

  9. How do mushrooms form? Does this involve asexual or sexual reproduction?
    •  Sexual reproduction. Hyphae of opposite mating types grow toward each other underground; their fusion produces numerous hyphae growing up toward the surface, forming a mushroom that releases spores.

  10. What is a mycosis?
    •  A fungal disease.

  11. Why are antifungal drugs difficult to develop?
    •  Because at the cellular level, human and fungal cells are biochemically and metabolically very similar — it's hard to find a difference between "your cell and the cell you're trying to kill" to exploit as a drug target (unlike bacteria, where peptidoglycan or the prokaryotic ribosome provide clear targets).

  12. How do fungal cells differ from human and animal cells?
    •  Fungal cells have a cell wall made of chitin (human cells have none).
    •  Fungal membranes use ergosterol to regulate fluidity; human membranes use cholesterol for the same role — structurally similar, but different compounds.

  13. What are the two most common cellular targets of antifungal medications?
    •  Ergosterol synthesis (cell membrane) and cell wall (chitin) synthesis.

  14. How does miconazole (Monistat) work?
    •  Inhibits ergosterol synthesis, disrupting the fungal cell membrane.

  15. How does anidulafungin (Eraxis) work?
    •  Inhibits cell wall (chitin) synthesis.

  16. Who discovered penicillin? How?
    •  Alexander Fleming. A plate of growing bacteria became contaminated with a Penicillium mold colony; he noticed bacterial cells near the mold were dying while cells farther away survived, suggesting the mold released a substance that killed the bacteria.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Unicellular / Multicellular, Heterotroph, Decomposer, Facultative anaerobe, Fermentation, Asexual/Sexual Reproduction, Binary Fission, Budding, Cell Wall — refreshers; see Q1–3 above and earlier modules.
  • Chitin — the tough polysaccharide (a polymer of glucosamine, similar to cellulose) making up the fungal cell wall; also found in arthropod exoskeletons (e.g., a cricket's covering, a shrimp shell).
  • Mycelium — the entire network of hyphae making up a mold.
  • Dimorphic — describes fungi able to grow in two different forms depending on conditions.
  • Cholesterol / Ergosterol — see Q12–13.

Microbe Spotlight - Candida albicans — yeast; causes vaginitis/yeast infection and thrush. - Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's/brewer's yeast. - Aspergillus — mold; causes "farmer's lung" from inhaled spores; produces conidiospores. - Penicillium — mold; P. chrysogenum is the source of penicillin; produces conidiospores. - Cryptococcus — associated with fungal meningitis. - Histoplasma — associated with histoplasmosis.

Disease Spotlight - Athlete's Foot, Ringworm, Thrush, Yeast Infection — common mycoses (e.g., from Candida albicans or dermatophyte molds). - Fungal Meningitis, Histoplasmosis — more serious mycoses (Cryptococcus, Histoplasma).

Scientist Spotlight - Alexander Fleming — discovered Penicillin (see Q16).
