Module 22: Specific Defenses — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. Who discovered humoral immunity? How?

    • Emil von Behring (Koch’s lab), following up on Roux’s diphtheria toxin work.
    • A guinea pig unexpectedly survived diphtheria toxin. He transferred its blood to another guinea pig, which was also immune → something in the blood gave immunity (lasting only ~3 weeks in the second animal).
    • He called it humoral immunity (blood was one of the four humors) and found the factor: antibodies. Nobel Prize 1901.

  2. What are antibodies (immunoglobulins; IG)?

    • Large glycoproteins that bind specific antigens; also called immunoglobulins (Ig) (globular immune proteins).
    • Produced by B cells (B lymphocytes); half-life about 3 weeks once released.

  3. What is the structure of an antibody? Constant and variable regions?

    • A Y-shaped, bilaterally symmetrical molecule of four polypeptide chains — 2 heavy + 2 light — held together by disulfide bonds.
    • Constant region (Fc): amino acid sequence the same within a class — the stem and part of the arms.
    • Variable region (V): at the tips of the arms; differs between antibodies and forms the antigen-binding sites (two identical sites per antibody).

  4. Do constant or variable regions determine an antibody’s class?

    • The constant region determines the class (IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, IgE).
    • Immune cells have receptors for the constant region — like a universal USB port / wall outlet that fits any antibody of that class regardless of what it recognizes.

  5. What is the function of an antibody’s variable regions?

    • They form the unique antigen-binding site — a lock-and-key fit with one specific antigen shape. This is where specificity/recognition happens.

  6. What are antigens? How do they interact with antibodies?

    • Antigen = a foreign substance (often on a pathogen’s surface) that binds an antibody and may elicit an immune response. Antigen ≠ pathogen: one bacterium can carry many different antigens.
    • Each antibody binds only its matching antigen shape in its variable region; with two binding sites, one antibody can bind two antigens at once.
    • Slides: name = ANTIbody GENerator; most are proteins, lipoproteins, glycoproteins, nucleoproteins, or polysaccharides/LPS.

  7. How do antibodies perform opsonization, agglutination, neutralization, complement activation, inflammation, and antibody-mediated cytotoxicity?

    • Opsonization: antibodies coat specific pathogens; phagocytes’ constant-region receptors grab them for phagocytosis.
    • Agglutination: two binding sites cross-link pathogens into clumps → a phagocyte engulfs many at once (raking leaves into a pile).
    • Neutralization: antibodies block attachment sites so pathogens (or toxins, e.g., snake venom) can’t bind host cells — solves the problem immediately.
    • Complement activation: adjacent antibodies bound to antigen activate complement specifically → opsonization, inflammation, MAC.
    • Inflammation: antigen binds antibodies (IgE) anchored on mast cells → histamine release.
    • Antibody-dependent cytotoxicity: antibodies coat large foreign cells (fungi, parasitic worms); eosinophils or NK cells bind the antibodies and release chemicals (perforins, granzymes — slides) that destroy the target.
    • These mechanisms happen at the same time — they aren’t mutually exclusive.

  8. How does each class function? (indicate which are monomers, dimers, pentamers) IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, IgE

    • IgG — monomer: most abundant (~80% of serum antibodies); works by most mechanisms except triggering mast cells; crosses the placenta.
    • IgM — pentamer when secreted: first antibody made after exposure; great at agglutination. Its monomer form sits on the B cell as the B cell receptor.
    • IgA — dimer: abundant in mucus, saliva, and breast milk; works mainly by neutralization.
    • IgD — monomer: few made; sometimes on B cell surfaces; function unclear.
    • IgE — monomer: on the surface of mast cells (mast cells have IgE-specific receptors) → triggers histamine/inflammation; involved in hypersensitivities (allergies).

  9. Which class of antibody is most abundant in blood serum? Why is the antibody above important during pregnancy?

    • IgG (~80%).
    • It crosses the placenta, giving the fetus the mother’s antibodies (natural passive immunity).

  10. Which class is abundant in mucus? How can it be shared by a mother with her newborn?

    • IgA (dimer) — mucus, saliva, breast milk.
    • Through breast milk after birth (once the umbilical cord is cut and IgG transfer ends).

  11. Who discovered clonal selection?

    • Frank Macfarlane Burnet (1950s), inspired by his hero Charles Darwin (natural selection; Galápagos finch beaks). Nobel Prize 1960.

  12. How does clonal selection occur?

    • Stem cells in the bone marrow give rise to billions of B cells, each with its own unique B cell receptor (like finches with different beaks).
    • An antigen arrives and binds the one B cell whose receptor fits → that B cell is selected.
    • The selected B cell clones itself (clonal expansion) into two “armies”: plasma cells and memory B cells.
    • Slides: protein antigens require helper T cells to activate B cells (T-dependent); non-protein antigens can activate B cells directly (T-independent) — see Module 23.

  13. What selects an immune cell to clone?

    • The antigen — specifically, the antigen binding to a matching B cell receptor.

  14. What are B cells and B cell receptors?

    • B cells = lymphocytes (white blood cells) that mature in the bone marrow and make antibodies.
    • B cell receptors = membrane-bound antibodies (IgM monomers) on the B cell surface; all have the same binding site, “advertising” (like a floor model) the one antibody that B cell can make.

  15. What are plasma cells and what are their functions?

    • Cloned B cells that secrete massive quantities of antibodies (lots of rough ER) with the same binding site as the selected B cell. They lack B cell receptors — they don’t need to recognize anything.

  16. What are memory B cells and what are their functions?

    • Cloned B cells that keep B cell receptors, don’t secrete antibodies now, and remain in circulation to make the response faster and stronger on a secondary exposure (“millions of needles in the haystack”).

  17. How do primary and secondary responses differ? (consider drawing a graph)

    • Graph: antibody titer (concentration) vs time.
    • Primary (initial) exposure: a lag of ~a week while the right B cell is found and cloned → you likely get sick. IgM rises first, then IgG (higher), then titers fall (half-life).
    • Secondary exposure: memory cells respond immediately; IgM about the same, but IgG is much higher and stays high longer → usually no disease. This is active immunity.

  18. What is meant by natural immunity and artificial immunity? Provide examples.

    • Natural: exposure through everyday life — natural active (getting infected and making your own antibodies) or natural passive (IgG across the placenta, IgA in breast milk).
    • Artificial: exposure by medical means — artificial active (vaccine = injected antigens) or artificial passive (injected preformed antibodies: antivenom, COVID monoclonal antibodies).

  19. What is meant by active immunity and passive immunity?

    • Active: your own B cells recognize the antigen and make plasma and memory cells → long-lasting (“permanent”).
    • Passive: you receive preformed antibodies; your B cells aren’t selected, so there’s no memory → temporary (lasts as long as the antibodies, ~3-week half-life) — but immediate.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Humoral (antibody-mediated) immunity — The B cell/antibody arm of the third line of defense; targets mainly extracellular pathogens (slides).
  • Glycoprotein — A protein with attached sugar groups — antibodies are large glycoproteins.
  • Eosinophil — WBC that, with NK cells, performs antibody-dependent cytotoxicity against large eukaryotic pathogens like worms.
  • Titer — Concentration of antibody in serum.
