Module 5: Metabolism — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is metabolism?
    •  The sum of all biochemical reactions occurring in a cell, of two types: catabolic and anabolic.

  2. What is catabolism, and what happens during catabolic reactions?
    •  The "decomposition" side of metabolism — larger molecules broken into smaller ones (Z → X + Y).
    •  Catabolic reactions are exergonic — they release energy.

  3. What is anabolism, and what happens during anabolic reactions?
    •  The "synthetic" side of metabolism — smaller molecules built into larger ones (A + B → C).
    •  Anabolic reactions are endergonic — they require energy.

  4. How do exergonic and endergonic reactions differ?
    •  Exergonic: releases energy, net negative free-energy change, spontaneous ("downhill").
    •  Endergonic: requires/absorbs energy, net positive free-energy change, not spontaneous on its own ("uphill").

  5. How do exergonic & endergonic reactions relate to catabolism & anabolism?
    •  Catabolic reactions are exergonic (breaking bonds releases energy).
    •  Anabolic reactions are endergonic (building bonds requires energy).

  6. What is meant by energy coupling?
    •  Energy released from an exergonic reaction is used to power/fuel an endergonic reaction, so the overall coupled process is still exergonic (spontaneous).

  7. Why is energy coupling necessary in cells?
    •  Many essential cellular (anabolic) reactions are endergonic and won't happen on their own; coupling them to an exergonic reaction (often ATP breakdown) supplies the needed energy.

  8. What is ATP, and how does ATP store energy?
    •  Adenosine Triphosphate. Energy is stored in the high-energy bond holding its third phosphate group; breaking that bond (ATP → ADP + phosphate) releases usable energy (~ -7.3 kcal/mol).

  9. What three components are used to build a single molecule of ATP?
    •  An adenine base, a ribose sugar (together = "adenosine"), and three phosphate groups.

  10. Why is ATP useful during energy coupling?
    •  More efficient energy coupling, a simple process to store/release energy (break one bond), and it's recyclable (ADP + P can be rebuilt into ATP).

  11. What is activation energy (Ea)?
    •  The energy required to initiate a reaction. Influences reaction rate: higher Ea → slower; lower Ea → faster.

  12. What are enzymes?
    •  Proteins with unique 3D shapes that catalyze specific biochemical reactions by lowering the activation energy requirement.

  13. How do enzymes catalyze biochemical reactions?
    •  By binding substrate at their active site and lowering the Ea needed, speeding the substrate-to-product conversion without being consumed.

  14. What is a substrate?
    •  The specific reactant molecule an enzyme binds/acts upon at its active site.

  15. How do substrate(s) relate to product(s)?
    •  The enzyme binds substrate(s) at the active site and converts them into product(s) as the reaction proceeds.

  16. What is the active site of an enzyme?
    •  The specific 3D region of the enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction is catalyzed.

  17. Which environmental factors affect enzyme activity, and what are their effects?
    •  Temperature, pH, and salt — all affect protein folding; extremes can cause denaturation (loss of shape/function).

  18. What is meant by feedback inhibition?
    •  (Negative) feedback inhibition: the product of a pathway inhibits an earlier enzyme in that same pathway, keeping product levels roughly constant.

  19. What is an allosteric site?
    •  A regulatory location on an enzyme, separate from the active site, where a molecule (e.g., the pathway's product, or a non-competitive inhibitor) can bind and change the enzyme's shape/activity.

  20. How does the allosteric site differ from an enzyme's active site?
    •  Active site = where substrate binds and the reaction happens. Allosteric site = separate regulatory spot where a different molecule binds to change enzyme shape/activity, without competing with the substrate.

  21. What role does the allosteric site play in feedback inhibition?
    •  The pathway's end-product binds the allosteric site, changing the enzyme's shape to inactive, halting further production until product levels drop.

  22. How does enzyme phosphorylation affect enzyme function?
    •  Adding a phosphate group can activate an inactive enzyme; removing the phosphate returns it to inactive — a reversible on/off switch.

  23. What is meant by competitive inhibition?
    •  An inhibitor structurally resembling the natural substrate binds directly to the active site, blocking the real substrate and inhibiting enzyme activity.

  24. How does competitive inhibition relate to antibiotic therapy for infections?
    •  Several antibiotics act as competitive inhibitors — mimicking a bacterial enzyme's natural substrate, binding its active site, and blocking a reaction bacteria need to survive (e.g., cell wall synthesis or folic acid synthesis).

  25. Which antibiotics (described in the lecture) act as competitive inhibitors?
    •  Penicillin (inhibits the enzyme for peptidoglycan cross-link/cell wall synthesis) and Sulfa drugs like sulfanilamide (inhibit the bacterial enzyme converting PABA into folic acid).

  26. How do sulfa drugs function, and why are they safe therapies for animals?
    •  Sulfa drugs (e.g., sulfanilamide) structurally resemble PABA and competitively bind the active site of the bacterial enzyme that converts PABA into folic acid, blocking its production. Without folic acid, bacteria can't make DNA, divide, or survive.
    •  Safe for humans/animals because we do not synthesize our own folic acid via that enzyme — we get folic acid from our diet, so the drug has nothing of ours to block.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Reactant — starting molecule(s) entering a reaction. Product — molecule(s) resulting from a reaction.
  • Catalyst — speeds up a reaction by lowering activation energy, without being permanently consumed; enzymes are biological catalysts.
  • Denaturation — loss of a protein's/enzyme's normal shape (from temperature, pH, or salt extremes), destroying its function.
  • Lock and Key Model and Induced Fit Model (flagged — study guide marks these "not in the recorded lectures"; verify testability with your professor) — models describing how an enzyme's active site fits its substrate.
  • Noncompetitive Inhibition (Allosteric Inhibition) — an inhibitor binds the allosteric site (not the active site) and changes the enzyme's shape, inhibiting its activity, without directly blocking the substrate from the active site.

Antibiotic Spotlight - Penicillin — competitively inhibits the enzyme responsible for peptidoglycan cross-linking. - Sulfa Drugs — competitively inhibit the PABA-to-folic-acid enzyme; safe for humans since we obtain folic acid from our diet.

Antiviral Spotlight - Tamiflu — per the instructor slides, an example (along with Methotrexate) of a drug that works as a competitive inhibitor; this type of inhibition is reversible — the inhibitor does not permanently alter the enzyme.
