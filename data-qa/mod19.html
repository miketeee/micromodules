Module 19: Principles of Disease — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. Refresher: What is pathogenesis?

    • The manner in which a disease develops — the second branch of pathology (etiology is the first).

  2. Refresher: What stages are associated with the development of an infection?

    • It takes a “perfect storm” to put you on the disease curve:
    • Reservoir → transmission → portal of entry → adherence → avoid host defenses → host damage → portal of exit.
    • Disease curve stages (Module 18): incubation → prodromal → illness → decline → convalescence.

  3. What is a reservoir of infection? Three kinds of reservoirs?

    • A continual source of infection — a place where the pathogen is already abundant.
    • Human (a sick person), animal (zoonoses), and non-living (water, soil, air).

  4. What are zoonoses? Examples? What are examples of non-living reservoirs?

    • Zoonosis = a disease that moves from an animal to a human. Ex: rabies (reservoirs: bats, skunks, foxes, dogs, raccoons).
    • Non-living reservoirs: contaminated water, soil, air.

  5. How do direct contact, indirect contact, and droplet transmission differ?

    • Direct contact: reservoir touches host — handshake, kissing, intercourse, patient care.
    • Indirect contact: a non-living fomite is the middleman — doorknob, cell phone, toys, medical equipment.
    • Droplet: sneeze, cough, talking, laughter within less than 1 meter. Beyond 1 m it counts as airborne (vehicle).

  6. What is a fomite? Examples?

    • A non-living object that carries pathogens between hosts in indirect contact: doorknobs, cell phones, daycare toys, medical equipment.

  7. What is vehicle transmission? Examples?

    • Transmission by something non-living that moves the pathogen:
    • Waterborne (contaminated well/creek; farm sewage downstream), foodborne (contaminated food shipped far away), airborne (> 1 meter).
    • Exposure to laboratory specimens (blood, urine) is also counted as vehicle transmission.

  8. What is vector transmission (name and distinguish between 2 types)?

    • A living arthropod carries the pathogen (vs a non-living vehicle).
    • Mechanical vector: carries pathogens on its body parts. Ex: housefly.
    • Biological vector: the pathogen reproduces inside the vector. Ex: Anopheles mosquito → Plasmodium (malaria).

  9. What are the 3 primary portals of entry? Which is most commonly used?

    • Broken skin (unbroken skin is one of our best defenses).
    • Mucous membranes — the most common portal; they line surfaces that exchange materials with the outside (digestive and respiratory tracts) → why respiratory infections are so common.
    • Parenteral route: something plunges through intact skin — injection, stab wound, rusty nail, bite or sting.

  10. What are HAIs? Why are healthcare facilities sources of infection?

    • Healthcare-associated infections (formerly nosocomial): infections a patient acquires at a healthcare facility — hospitals, clinics, dental offices, nursing homes, PT facilities. About 15% of patients get one, and the number is rising.
    • Why: many reservoirs (sick patients), many invasive procedures (catheters, surgery, dressing changes) = transmission opportunities, and many compromised hosts (predisposing factors like immunosuppressive drugs, burns).

  11. What are some organisms commonly associated with HAIs?

    • Most common HAIs: UTIs and surgical-site infections.
    • Staphylococcus aureus — the “problem child,” especially MRSA (methicillin-resistant, multidrug-resistant).
    • Also C. difficile, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (the big problem in the 1980s), Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterococcus.

  12. What precautions can help to prevent HAIs?

    • Hand washing (most effective) — between patients and procedures.
    • PPE — gloves, masks, gowns.
    • Aseptic technique — culturing, specimen handling, wound dressing.
    • Isolation (quarantine) — keep compromised hosts away from reservoirs (burn ward away from infectious patients; sick vs well side of the waiting room).
    • Education — the plain name “healthcare-associated infection” helps public awareness.

  13. Why must organisms adhere to host tissues to cause disease? How do they adhere (discuss 3 bacterial structures that allow for adherence)?

    • To avoid being washed away by sneezing, coughing, urination, defecation, vomiting (why UTI patients are told to drink fluids).
    • Usually a binding event: a pathogen molecule binds a receptor on the host cell.
    • Fimbriae: polymerized protein hairs → E. coli on bladder cells.
    • Capsule: sticky carbohydrate coat → biofilms; Streptococcus mutans on teeth.
    • M-protein: tiny protein hairs → Streptococcus pyogenes in the throat.
    • Invasins: let bacteria enter host cells → Salmonella typhimurium (can’t be washed away from inside a cell).

  14. What are biofilms? Examples? Why are biofilms relevant to healthcare & HAIs?

    • A thin layer of life — many bacteria adhering to a surface, often using a capsule.
    • Examples: S. mutans on teeth, contact lenses, catheters.
    • Catheters left in too long grow biofilms → the main culprit behind UTIs, the most common HAI.

  15. What are M-proteins? Example?

    • Very short protein hairs (smaller than fimbriae) that bind host receptors for adherence and also inhibit phagocytosis. Ex: Streptococcus pyogenes (strep throat).

  16. How do pathogens avoid host defenses?

    • They must avoid defenses such as phagocytosis (white blood cells eating them) and antibodies (proteins that mark them for disposal).
    • Tools: capsule, M-protein, invasins, coagulase, kinase, IgA protease.

  17. How do capsules and M-proteins contribute to avoiding host defenses?

    • Both inhibit phagocytosis — the white blood cell can’t grab the slippery cell (“greased pig”).
    • Capsule ex: Streptococcus pneumoniae — encapsulated strains cause pneumonia; strains without a capsule are fairly harmless.
    • M-protein ex: Streptococcus pyogenes.

  18. What are invasins and IgA proteases? How does each contribute to avoiding host defenses?

    • Invasins: secreted substances that let bacteria move through the host cell membrane into the cell → hidden from phagocytes and antibodies. Ex: Salmonella typhimurium.
    • IgA protease: an enzyme that destroys IgA antibodies, which are most abundant in mucus/mucous membranes. Ex: Streptococcus pneumoniae; the gonorrhea bacterium also needs it.

  19. What do coagulase and kinase enzymes do? How do they contribute to avoiding host defenses? Examples?

    • Coagulase clots blood → the pathogen walls itself inside a clot so white blood cells can’t get in. Ex: S. aureus (coagulase-positive; MRSA infections).
    • Kinase digests clotted blood → when white blood cells trap the infection inside a clot, the pathogen dissolves it and escapes. Ex: S. pyogenes (kinase-positive).

  20. How does direct cell lysis harm a host?

    • Bacteria that use invasins enter host cells, multiply by binary fission inside, and the host cell lyses (bursts) and dies, releasing the bacteria. Ex: Salmonella typhimurium → severe GI disease.
    • Host damage is what separates pathogens from normal flora (which go through all the other steps too).

  21. What are siderophores? How do they harm a host?

    • Substances that bind host nutrients — usually iron — very strongly, leaching it out of red blood cells and other cells.
    • RBCs lose oxygen-carrying ability; enzymes lose their iron cofactors. Ex: Yersinia pestis (the Plague).

  22. How do collagenase and hyaluronidase damage host tissue?

    • They digest collagen and hyaluronic acid, the extracellular matrix of connective tissue — including the connective tissue of blood vessels feeding the area → tissue dies (necrosis) → gangrene.
    • Ex: Clostridium perfringens (common cause of gangrene; Civil War wounds; diabetic feet).

  23. Who discovered bacterial exotoxins? How?

    • Émile Roux (Pasteur’s lab) — diphtheria toxin.
    • Diphtheria bacteria stay in the throat yet cause body-wide effects → he suspected a toxin. He filtered a broth culture and injected the cell-free filtrate into guinea pigs.
    • At 24–48 h of culture nothing happened; after letting the culture grow ~2 weeks, tiny doses killed the guinea pigs (their livers are poor at removing toxins).

  24. What are other names for Type I, Type II, and Type III exotoxins? How does each damage a host? Examples?

    • Exotoxins = protein toxins secreted mostly by Gram-positive bacteria; often from lysogenic conversion (prophage genes).
    • Type I = superantigens: cause an excessive immune response (very high fever, shock, vomiting, diarrhea). Ex: S. aureus → toxic shock syndrome.
    • Type II = hemolysins: lyse red blood cells (often to get nutrients). Ex: S. pyogenes.
    • Type III = A-B toxins: B subunit binds (and may enter) the host cell; A (active) subunit is toxic — inhibits protein synthesis or acts as a neurotoxin. Ex: Clostridium botulinum → botulism (blocks neuron→muscle signals → paralysis); Botox (wrinkles, migraines, excess sweating).

  25. What are endotoxins? What kind of microorganisms are associated with them? How do endotoxins damage a host?

    • Toxic substances that are part of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria — not secreted.
    • Ex: lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer leaflet of the outer membrane.
    • Released when the bacterium dies and breaks apart; in blood/tissues they trigger an excessive immune response (fever, blood-pressure drop/shock, vomiting) — similar to superantigens.

  26. How do portals of exit relate to portals of entry? Why is awareness of both kinds of portals important medically?

    • The exit portal is usually the same as the portal of entry (with exceptions).
    • Once the pathogen exits, the host has become a reservoir and the cycle begins again — knowing the portals tells you how to block transmission.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Healthcare-associated infection (HAI) — Formerly ‘nosocomial’; see Q10–12.
  • Phagocytosis — ‘Cellular eating’ — white blood cells engulf pathogens.
  • IgA antibodies — Antibodies most abundant in mucus and mucous membranes; destroyed by IgA protease.
  • Virulence factor — Any characteristic, molecule, or structure that lets a microbe cause disease (slides): fimbriae, capsules, M-protein, invasins, coagulase, kinase, IgA protease, siderophores, collagenase, hyaluronidase, toxins, LPS.
  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — Lipid + sugar chain in the Gram-negative outer membrane; the classic endotoxin.
