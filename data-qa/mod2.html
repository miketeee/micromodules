Module 2: Introduction to Microbiology (Part 2) — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What helpful roles do microorganisms play in the environment?
    •  Oxygen production via photosynthesis (cyanobacteria).
    •  Decomposition and nutrient recycling (bacteria and fungi).

  2. What are cyanobacteria, and why are they important?
    •  Photosynthetic bacteria (e.g., Anabaena variabilis), found almost anywhere there is moisture.
    •  They perform photosynthesis (CO2 + H2O → Glucose + O2) and are a major contributor to the oxygen in our air (~20% O2).

  3. What are decomposers, and why are they important?
    •  Bacteria and fungi that break down dead organic material.
    •  Important for nutrient recycling — returning nutrients to the ecosystem.

  4. How are microbes used in food production? (examples)
    •  Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugar, producing CO2 gas (leavened bread) and ethanol (beer, wine).
    •  Bacteria are used to make yogurt and cheese.

  5. How does Lactobacillus benefit health?
    •      Lactobacillus acidophilus* is a rod-shaped bacterium that produces lactic acid.
    •  It is a normal, beneficial ("good") resident of the vaginal environment and acts as a     probiotic — it helps *prevent yeast infections.

  6. What is genetic engineering?
    •  A biotechnology technique of manipulating an organism's genes so it produces a desired protein product (e.g., bacteria engineered to produce insulin).

  7. When was the Golden Age of Microbiology?
    •  A roughly 50-year period, from the mid-1800s to the early 1900s.

  8. What questions did scientists try to answer in the Golden Age of Microbiology?
    •  Does life arise spontaneously?
    •  What causes fermentation?
    •  What causes disease?
    •  How can disease be prevented/treated?

  9. What was the theory of spontaneous generation?
    •  The (incorrect) belief that living organisms could arise directly from non-living matter, without coming from other living organisms.

  10. Which scientists contributed to disproving spontaneous generation, and what were their contributions?
    •  Francesco Redi — suspected microorganisms might cause meat spoilage.
    •  Lazzaro Spallanzani — showed boiling sterilizes broth; broth stayed sterile in a sealed flask but grew microbes in an open flask.
    •  Louis Pasteur — repeated the experiment with swan-necked flasks, finally disproving spontaneous generation for good.

  11. How did Pasteur's experiment differ from Spallanzani's experiment?
    •  Pasteur used a swan-necked flask rather than a fully sealed one.
    •  Air (and the hypothesized "vital force") could still enter, but gravity trapped incoming microorganisms in the bend of the neck — so the broth remained sterile, proving microorganisms (not air) caused contamination.

  12. How did Pasteur's work on fermentation lead to the Germ Theory of Disease?
    •  Pasteur showed that a living microorganism (yeast) causes fermentation, and that bacteria cause spoilage.
    •  This established that specific living microbes cause specific biological/chemical effects — the conceptual bridge to microbes causing disease.

  13. Which two scientists developed the Germ Theory of Disease?
    •  Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch.

  14. What contributions did each scientist make to the Germ Theory?
    •  Pasteur — proposed that microorganisms cause disease (built on his fermentation work).
    •  Koch — performed the rigorous experimental proof, in the 1870s.

  15. What contributions toward combatting microorganisms were made by:
    •  Ignaz Semmelweis — emphasized the importance of hand washing.
    •  Florence Nightingale — promoted hospital cleanliness.
    •  Joseph Lister — pioneered the development of germicides.
    •  Alexander Fleming — discovered the first antibiotic, Penicillin.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Fermentation — the process by which sugars are converted into alcohol (and CO2) by microorganisms such as yeast.
  • Pasteurization — heating a liquid to kill/reduce microorganisms for preservation; developed by Pasteur.
  • Enzyme — a protein that catalyzes chemical reactions; discovered by Eduard Buchner, who showed fermentation could occur using only yeast extract (enzymes), without living yeast cells.
  • Germicide — a substance that kills germs; pioneered by Joseph Lister.
  • Antibiotic — a substance that kills/inhibits bacteria; the first was Penicillin (Fleming).

Microbe Spotlight - Lactobacillus acidophilus — rod-shaped, lactic-acid-producing probiotic bacterium found in the vaginal environment. - Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's/brewer's yeast; ferments sugar into CO2 and ethanol.

Scientist Spotlight (contributions not already listed above) - Eduard Buchner — performed fermentation with yeast extract alone; discovered enzymes. - Robert Hooke, Anton van Leeuwenhoek — see Module 1 (pioneers of microscopy/microorganism discovery).
