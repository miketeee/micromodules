Module 7: Microbial Growth — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is a mixed culture?
    •  A growth environment (e.g., a liquid broth) containing multiple different species of bacteria together.

  2. What is a pure culture? Importance?
    •  A population of organisms all descended from a single cell (one species/strain).
    •  Important because it lets scientists study, identify, and test one specific organism without interference from others.

  3. How was pure-culturing discovered?
    •  Robert Koch spread mixed cultures onto potato slices; isolated colonies (visible piles of one species) formed in separate spots, showing that physically separating cells allowed a single species to grow into a "pure" colony.

  4. Who pioneered growing bacteria in pure culture?
    •  Robert Koch.

  5. How was the use of agar in growth media discovered?
    •  Koch's earlier gelatin-based solid media melted at human body temperature (37°C), so it couldn't culture human pathogens.
    •  Fannie Hesse (wife of lab member Walther Hesse) used a soup thickener called agar-agar (from red algae) in cooking and noticed it solidified without melting at body temperature. Her husband Walther Hesse brought it to Koch's lab, giving rise to agar-based solid media.

  6. What are the advantages of solid and liquid media?
    •  Solid media (agar plates) let you visually isolate and separate individual colonies (pure cultures).
    •  Liquid (broth) media let bacteria grow in large, well-mixed quantities, but make it harder to visually separate species.

  7. What are streak plates? How do they produce pure cultures?
    •  A technique (developed in Koch's lab, using an inoculating loop) that drags a diminishing sample of a mixed culture across four quadrants of an agar plate, progressively diluting and spreading the cells.
    •  By the last quadrant, cells are spread far enough apart that each grows into its own separate, pure colony.

  8. What is aseptic technique?
    •  Practices (pioneered by Robert Koch) used to prevent contamination of cultures, instruments, and samples by unwanted microorganisms.

  9. What are photoautotrophs and chemoheterotrophs?
    •  Photoautotrophs harness sunlight via photosynthesis to build their own glucose.
    •  Chemoheterotrophs must obtain energy molecules (e.g., glucose) directly from the environment.

  10. What are psychrophiles, mesophiles, thermophiles, and hyperthermophiles? Which category includes most pathogenic organisms?
    •  Psychrophiles (-5 to 20°C), Mesophiles (20 to 45°C), Thermophiles (45 to 70°C), Hyperthermophiles (70 to 110°C).
    •  Most pathogens are Mesophiles, since human body temperature (37°C) falls in that range.

  11. What are obligate aerobes, obligate anaerobes, facultative anaerobes, aerotolerant anaerobes, and microaerophiles? Example of each?
    •  Obligate aerobes — must have O2 (Pseudomonas aeruginosa).
    •  Facultative anaerobes — prefer O2 but can live without it (Escherichia coli).
    •  Obligate anaerobes — cannot tolerate O2 (Clostridium botulinum).
    •  Aerotolerant anaerobes — don't use O2 but can survive in its presence (Streptococcus pyogenes).
    •  Microaerophiles — prefer low levels of O2 (Helicobacter pylori).

  12. How can oxygen requirements be determined experimentally?
    •  By creating an O2 concentration gradient (e.g., in a reducing/thioglycolate broth tube — high O2 near the top, low/no O2 near the bottom) and inoculating the organism, then observing where in the gradient it grows.

  13. What are neutrophiles, acidophiles, and alkaliphiles? Which category includes most pathogenic organisms?
    •  Neutrophiles (pH 5–8), Acidophiles (pH <5), Alkaliphiles (pH >8).
    •  Most human pathogens are Neutrophiles, since human body pH (~7.35) falls in that range.

  14. What are microenvironments?
    •  Small sub-environments with conditions that differ from the surrounding macro-environment.

  15. How does Helicobacter pylori create a microenvironment?
    •  Although a neutrophile, H. pylori survives the acidic stomach (pH 2) by producing the enzyme urease, which converts urea into CO2 and ammonia (NH3); the ammonia locally raises the pH around the bacterium, creating a more neutral microenvironment.

  16. What is generation time? Binary fission?
    •  Generation (doubling) time — the time required for a bacterial population to double (e.g., ~30 minutes under ideal conditions).
    •  Binary fission — the asexual reproduction process bacteria use to divide one cell into two identical cells.

  17. What is the growth rate equation? How is it used?
    •  Nt = N(t=0) × 2ⁿ, where Nt = cells at time t, N(t=0) = starting cell count, and n = number of doublings during time t (based on generation time).
    •  Used to calculate population size after a period of exponential growth.

  18. What are the stages of a bacterial growth curve? Describe each.
    •  Lag phase — very slow, hard-to-detect growth (population still small).
    •  Log (exponential) phase — rapid doubling; population grows steeply.
    •  Stationary phase — division rate = death rate, so population size levels off (most natural populations, like normal body microbiota, sit here).
    •  Death phase — death rate exceeds division rate, population declines (e.g., antibiotics/immune system killing off a pathogen, or a lab culture running out of nutrients/space).

  19. How do bacterial growth curves relate to signs and symptoms of disease?
    •  A pathogen's growth phase in the body can track with disease progression: in stationary phase, bacterial numbers (and effects) are balanced/stable (as with normal microbiota); a shift into death phase — triggered by antibiotics or the immune system killing bacteria faster than they divide — corresponds to the infection being brought under control and symptoms resolving.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Agar — the algae-derived solidifying agent used in solid growth media (see Q5).
  • Solid Media / Liquid Media — see Q6.
  • Reducing media / Thioglycolate broth — media used to create an oxygen gradient for testing oxygen requirements (see Q12).

Scientist Spotlight - Robert Koch — pioneered pure culturing; developed the pour plate and streak plate methods and aseptic technique. - Julius Richard Petri — designed the Petri dish (originally sized for a potato slice) for Koch's lab. - Fannie Hesse & Walther Hesse — introduced agar to solidify growth media (see Q5).

Microbe Spotlight - Serratia marcescens — example organism referenced in the pure-culturing/colony discussion. - Pseudomonas aeruginosa — obligate aerobe. - Escherichia coli — facultative anaerobe. - Clostridium botulinum — obligate anaerobe. - Streptococcus pyogenes — aerotolerant anaerobe. - Helicobacter pylori — microaerophile; neutrophile that creates a microenvironment via urease.
