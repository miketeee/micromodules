Module 17: Viruses — Part 3 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is meant by positive sense single-stranded viral RNA (+ ssRNA)?

    • Also called “sense” RNA — its sequence can be directly translated into protein by ribosomes (it acts like mRNA). Nothing to do with electrical charge.
    • Ex: coronavirus.

  2. What is meant by negative sense single-stranded viral RNA (- ssRNA)?

    • Also called “antisense” RNA — complementary to + sense RNA, so it cannot be directly translated; it must first be transcribed into a complementary + strand.

  3. Which viruses have reverse transcriptase?

    • Retroviruses — e.g., HIV (and FIV, feline immunodeficiency virus). The enzyme is carried inside the virion.

  4. What is reverse transcriptase and what does it do?

    • A viral enzyme that reverse-transcribes RNA into DNA — it converts the retrovirus’s single-stranded RNA genome into double-stranded DNA (“retro” = backward from normal transcription).

  5. What occurs when a retrovirus infects a host cell? Provide an example virus.

    • Ex: HIV.
    • Entry by membrane fusion → uncoating.
    • Reverse transcriptase converts the +ssRNA genome into dsDNA.
    • The dsDNA integrates into the host chromosome as a provirus (copied whenever the cell divides).
    • Biosynthesis: the provirus is transcribed into new RNA genomes and mRNAs; mRNAs are translated into viral proteins (including spike proteins).
    • Release by budding — slow budding lets the cell survive (persistent infection); mass budding destroys it.

  6. What are the three methods used to cultivate eukaryotic viruses in a lab?

    • Viruses only grow inside living cells (bacteriophages are easy — just grow bacteria).
    • 1. Live animals (e.g., mice) — costly, messy, ethical concerns.
    • 2. Embryonated (fertilized) eggs — cheap and self-contained; inject amniotic fluid, embryo, or yolk sac. Influenza for vaccines grows here (why flu shots ask about egg allergies).
    • 3. Cell (tissue) cultures — needed when a virus only grows in specific cell types (e.g., HIV); costly, time-consuming passaging.

  7. Who was the first to link a virus with cancer in animals? How?

    • Peyton Rous (1911): removed a sarcoma (muscle-tissue cancer) from a chicken, ground it with sand, passed it through a filter, and injected the cell-free filtrate into a young chicken, which developed sarcoma → the Rous sarcoma virus. Nobel Prize 1966.

  8. Which two scientists first linked a virus with cancer in humans?

    • Michael Anthony Epstein and Yvonne Barr (~1964) — discovered the Epstein-Barr virus, linked to lymphoma and other human cancers.

  9. How do proto-oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes normally function?

    • Proto-oncogenes encode proteins that turn ON cell division (the gas pedal).
    • Tumor-suppressor genes encode proteins that turn OFF cell division (the brakes) — ex: p53.
    • Together they keep the cell cycle (G1 → S → G2 → M) balanced so cells divide only when they should.

  10. What can happen to a cell if these genes are not functioning normally?

    • The cell cycle runs out of control — cells keep dividing without stopping in G1 → uncontrolled cell growth/division → a tumor; with accumulated mutations, cancer.

  11. What is the p53 protein and what does it normally do in cells?

    • The protein encoded by the p53 tumor-suppressor gene — the “guardian angel” protein.
    • Regulates (stops) cell division and helps manage damaged/mutated DNA.
    • p53 mutations occur in ~60% of all human cancers.

  12. How can viruses lead to tumor formation?

    • Some viruses produce proteins that inactivate the p53 protein — taking the brakes off → uncontrolled cell division (tumor).
    • Without p53, the cell also accumulates DNA mutations, which get passed on — the transition from uncontrolled growth to cancer.

  13. What are examples of viruses that are able to cause cancer (i.e. oncoviruses)?

    • Human papillomavirus (HPV) — cervical cancer.
    • Hepatitis B and C viruses — liver cancer.
    • Epstein-Barr virus — lymphoma and other human cancers.
    • Rous sarcoma virus — sarcoma in chickens.

  14. Which virus is associated with cervical cancer? What other cancers can this virus cause?

    • Human papillomavirus (HPV); detected early by the Pap smear.
    • Other: textbook (Ch. 6) lists vulvar and vaginal cancer (plus genital warts/papillomas). Other HPV-linked cancers aren’t named in the provided sources — check with your professor if the exam expects more.

  15. What are HeLa cells?

    • An immortal line of cancer cells grown from a cervical-cancer biopsy taken from Henrietta Lacks (1920–1951) at Johns Hopkins; her cancer was caused by HPV.
    • Extremely aggressive, rapidly dividing cells that never stop dividing (normal cells divide ~50 times in culture, then die). Taken without her knowledge or consent.

  16. How have HeLa cells been used for scientific and biomedical research?

    • The most-studied human cells: shipped to labs worldwide; textbook: the first continuous cell line, establishing tissue culture for cell biology, virology, and medicine.
    • Used in research on polio, cancer, and AIDS, and in developing many drugs and treatments — generating billions of dollars, while her family received no compensation (ethics issue; book: The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks).

  17. For each viral genome type, describe the process of biosynthesis: How is the genome replicated? How is the genome used to make proteins?

    • dsDNA (ex: HPV) — Replicated by normal DNA replication; proteins: genome transcribed into mRNA, then translated.
    • ssDNA — host enzymes synthesize a complementary strand → dsDNA, which serves as a template to make new ssDNA genomes and to transcribe mRNA for proteins.
    • dsRNA (ex: rotavirus) — viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase uses the − strand as a template to make +ssRNA, which is translated into proteins; + strands also serve as templates for new genome strands. (Textbook, Ch. 6.)
    • +ssRNA (ex: coronavirus) — proteins: genome is translated directly (acts as mRNA); replicated by first making a complementary −ssRNA template, which is copied into many new +ssRNA genomes.
    • −ssRNA (ex: influenza, textbook) — proteins: must first be transcribed into +ssRNA (by viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase), then translated; replicated by using that + strand as a template to make new −ssRNA genomes.
    • ssRNA-RT (retrovirus, ex: HIV) — reverse transcriptase makes dsDNA from the +ssRNA genome → integrates as a provirus → host transcribes the provirus into new +ssRNA genomes and mRNAs (translated into proteins).
    • Key rule (slides): a template is always required — even viruses are bound by the Central Dogma.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Oncovirus — A cancer-causing virus (Q12–13). Not all viruses cause cancer, and not all cancers are viral.
  • Cancer — Unregulated/uncontrolled cell growth and division that forms tumors; becomes true cancer as mutations accumulate.
  • Cell cycle refreshers — G1 = cell does its normal job; S = DNA synthesis (replication); G2 = second growth/prep phase; M = mitosis. Regulated by proto-oncogenes (go) and tumor-suppressor genes (stop).
  • p53 gene / p53 protein — See Q11.
  • Virus Spotlight — Feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV) — A retrovirus of cats (cat version of HIV).
  • Virus Spotlight — Coronavirus — +ssRNA virus (Q1, Q17).
  • Oncovirus Spotlight — Epstein-Barr (lymphoma), Rous sarcoma virus (chicken sarcoma), Hepatitis B & C (liver cancer), HPV (cervical cancer).
  • Scientist Spotlight — Dmitri Ivanovsky — discovered viruses with the filter method that Rous later copied; Peyton Rous (Q7); Michael Anthony Epstein & Yvonne Barr (Q8).
  • History Spotlight — Henrietta Lacks / HeLa cells — See Q15–16.
