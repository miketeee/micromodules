Module 21: Nonspecific Defenses — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is the complement system?

    • A group of blood serum proteins (slides: 30+) that circulate in an inactive state and can be activated to defend against pathogens.
    • Activation is a cascade — like dominoes: activating one protein (often by splitting it, e.g., C3 → C3a + C3b) activates the next.
    • Mostly a nonspecific (second-line) defense, but it bridges nonspecific and specific immunity because antibodies can trigger it too.

  2. What can activate the complement system (three things)?

    • Mannose and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — common microbial molecules → nonspecific triggers.
    • Antibodies → a specific trigger.

  3. What three things happen when complement is activated?

    • Opsonization — coating microbes to enhance phagocytosis.
    • Inflammation — triggering mast cells to release histamine.
    • Membrane attack complex (MAC) — punching pores that lyse the microbe (cytolysis).

  4. What is mannose? Where is it found?

    • A very common sugar on the surface of bacteria (many species), so recognizing it is nonspecific. It can trigger complement and is recognized by Toll-like receptors on macrophages (Module 20).

  5. What is endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide, or LPS)? Where is it found?

    • LPS = a lipid + sugar-chain molecule in the outer membrane (outer leaflet) of Gram-negative bacteria — the classic endotoxin (Module 19). Released when Gram-negative cells die; causes excessive immune responses.
    • Because many bacteria have it, it is a nonspecific complement trigger.

  6. What is opsonization? How does complement activation lead to opsonization?

    • Opsonization = coating a microbe to mark it for disposal and enhance phagocytosis (opsonize = “to cater/prepare for eating”).
    • Activated C3 splits into C3a and C3b; C3b coats the bacteria. Macrophages have C3b receptors, which help them grab the microbe and wrap pseudopods around it.

  7. How does activated complement trigger inflammation?

    • C3b activates (splits) C5 → C5a + C5b.
    • C3a and C5a bind receptors on mast cells → mast cells release histamine → inflammation (vasodilation, leaky vessels, redness, heat, swelling, pain, WBCs arrive).
    • Same outcome as Module 20 — just a different trigger (complement instead of tissue damage).

  8. What is the MAC (membrane attack complex)? What does it do?

    • C5b activates C6, which recruits C7, C8, and C9; together they insert into the bacterial membrane, and C9 forms a ring-shaped pore (channel).
    • Several pores → the bacterium lyses (bursts) = cytolysis.

  9. What are cytokines? List several examples.

    • Small proteins released by host cells that regulate the immune response — like little hormones controlling immune cells.
    • Examples: interleukins (“between the whites” — released by WBCs; some are pyrogens) and interferons (antiviral). Slides: hundreds exist — pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, vasodilators/constrictors, growth factors.

  10. What chemicals generate a fever and what kind of cytokines are they?

    • Pyrogens (“fire generators”) — some interleukins act as pyrogenic cytokines.
    • Slides: endogenous pyrogens come from within the body; some pathogens make exogenous pyrogens.

  11. How is a fever generated? How does a fever defend against disease?

    • Pyrogenic interleukins reach the hypothalamus and reset the body’s thermostat higher.
    • Human and bacterial enzymes both peak near 37 °C, but bacterial enzyme activity falls off more steeply — a low-grade fever denatures bacterial enzymes while ours still work.
    • A high fever (~44–46 °C) is potentially fatal because our enzymes denature too — so don’t rush to eliminate a mild fever.
    • Slides add: fever limits nutrient access for bacteria and boosts host metabolism and immune reactions.

  12. How does interferon defend against disease?

    • A nonspecific antiviral cytokine produced and secreted by virus-infected cells.
      1. A virus infects a cell and starts making new viruses. 2. The infected cell makes and secretes interferon. 3. Interferon binds receptors on neighboring cells, which make antiviral proteins (AVPs). 4. When released viruses enter those cells and make viral RNA, AVPs destroy the RNA → no successful infection.
    • It is a local response; interferon can also stimulate natural killer cells.

  13. Does interferon protect the cell that makes it (explain)?

    • No — the infected cell’s fate is sealed (it will likely lyse). Interferon warns and protects neighboring cells.

  14. Why does interferon used to treat disease cause harmful side effects?

    • AVPs destroy RNA in general, not just viral RNA — so cells making AVPs shut down their own protein production.
    • Injected interferon is spread everywhere by the circulatory system, so cells all over the body make AVPs → significant side effects. Local delivery would be better.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Serum — The liquid portion of blood (after clotting); complement proteins circulate in it.
  • Cytolysis — Bursting of a cell — the result of the membrane attack complex.
  • Antigen / antibodies / NK cells — Mentioned here; described in Modules 22–23.
