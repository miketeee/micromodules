Module 20: Nonspecific Defenses — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. Who discovered phagocytes and phagocytosis? How?

    • Élie (Eli) Metchnikoff. Combining Pasteur/Koch’s germ theory with Darwin’s evolution, he reasoned that if microbes evolve to cause disease, hosts must evolve defenses.
    • He poked bacteria into transparent starfish larvae with a rose thorn and watched cells crawl over and eat the bacteria → named them phagocytes (eating cells) and the process phagocytosis. Nobel Prize 1908.
    • Critics first thought the cells were parasitic amoebas. He also founded the idea of probiotics (buttermilk / Lactobacillus acidophilus in a long-lived Italian dairy village).

  2. What distinguishes innate from adaptive defenses? Which is nonspecific (define)? Specific (define)?

    • Innate = nonspecific: inborn (present from birth), does not target a specific antigen, rapid, and does not improve with repeated exposure. Ex: fever.
    • Adaptive (acquired) = specific: develops through life, targets a specific antigen, slower, and improves with each exposure (memory). Only vertebrates have it.
    • Antigen = a foreign substance (e.g., on a pathogen’s surface) that triggers an immune reaction.

  3. What are first-, second-, and third-line defenses? Provide examples for each and indicate which are nonspecific and/or specific.

    • First line (innate, nonspecific): physical barriers (skin, mucous membranes, ciliary escalator, lacrimal apparatus), chemical/antimicrobial substances (sebum, lysozyme, gastric juice…), and resident microbiota.
    • Second line (mostly innate, nonspecific): inflammation, phagocytosis, fever, complement, natural killer cells (plus interferon and other cytokines).
    • Third line (adaptive, specific): antibody-mediated (B cells, extracellular pathogens) and cell-mediated (T cells, intracellular pathogens) immunity — where memory lives.

  4. What physical barriers are involved with immunity? Which is the best, most effective and protective physical barrier? What defensive features are associated with it? How do each of these features protect the host?

    • Physical barriers: intact skin, mucous membranes (mucus), ciliary escalator, lacrimal apparatus.
    • Intact skin is the best barrier — the main reason you aren’t sick right now.
    • Dry → too little moisture for bacteria. Salty (sweat evaporates, leaving salt) → most bacteria can’t tolerate it. Acidic (pH ~3–5 from fatty acids in sebum) → denatures enzymes. Constant shedding (exfoliation) carries microbes away. Normal flora (S. epidermidis) outcompetes pathogens like S. aureus.

  5. Where are mucous membranes located? How does mucus provide protection?

    • They line surfaces that exchange materials with the outside: respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts (and eyes — slides).
    • Goblet cells secrete sticky mucus that traps microbes; the mucosa also hosts normal flora (L. acidophilus).
    • The mucus is flushed by sneezing, coughing, vomiting, diarrhea, urination, and the ciliary escalator.

  6. What is the ciliary escalator and how does it contribute to defense?

    • Cilia on cells lining the respiratory tract beat like a car-wash brush, moving mucus and trapped microbes/debris at about 1–3 cm per hour up the trachea to the throat → swallowed → destroyed by stomach acid.
    • Smoking destroys it → smoker’s cough (coughing is the only way left to clear mucus). Slides: Bordetella pertussis (whooping cough) toxin destroys it too.

  7. How do resident microbiota (normal flora) protect body surfaces from infection? Where is S. epidermidis located and what defensive role does it play? Where is L. acidophilus located and what are its defensive roles?

    • Through microbial antagonism: competing for nutrients, occupying space, and creating unfavorable conditions (acids, antimicrobial compounds).
    • S. epidermidis — on the skin; outcompetes S. aureus (more S. epidermidis = fewer staph infections).
    • L. acidophilus — in the digestive and reproductive (vaginal) mucous membranes; makes acid (vaginal pH 3–5) → inhibits Candida albicans; antibiotics that kill it → yeast infection.

  8. Which 8 fluids protect body surfaces? Identify chemicals associated with each. How does each chemical affect microorganisms? How does washing action contribute to defense (examples)?

    • Sebum (sebaceous glands): fatty acids → skin pH 3–5 (denatures enzymes); also lubricates skin so it doesn’t crack into a portal of entry.
    • Lysozyme (in saliva, sweat, tears, urine): digests peptidoglycan (mainly Gram-positive cell walls).
    • Saliva: lysozyme + IgA antibodies, pH ~6.5.
    • Sweat: lysozyme + salt (salty residue when it dries).
    • Gastric juice: HCl, pH ~2 (slides 2–3) — like battery acid; destroys most microbes.
    • Vaginal secretions: lactic acid from L. acidophilus, pH 3–5.
    • Urine: lysozyme, pH ~6; its flow flushes the urinary tract.
    • Earwax: sebum/fatty acids, pH ~3–5.
    • (Tears — via the lacrimal apparatus — also carry lysozyme.) Washing action: tears + blinking (“windshield wipers”), urination, sneezing, coughing, vomiting, and diarrhea physically flush microbes away.

  9. What causes inflammation?

    • Tissue damage — either directly (e.g., a blade punctures skin and introduces microbes) or indirectly in response to infection (toxins, lysed cells).
    • Damaged mast cells release histamine, which triggers the inflammatory response. (Histamine can also be released in response to complement and antibodies.)

  10. What are the functions of mast cells and histamine in inflammation?

    • Mast cells — “mall cops” scattered through tissues — hold vesicles full of histamine and release it when tissue is damaged, sounding the alarm.
    • Histamine is a vasodilator: vessels widen → more blood flow (redness, heat) and lower blood pressure; widened vessels become leaky → fluid accumulates (swelling, pain) and WBCs crawl out (diapedesis) to the site.

  11. What are neutrophils? Monocytes? Macrophages?

    • Neutrophils: first responders; begin phagocytosis immediately and eat so much they die (most common WBC — slides).
    • Monocytes: arrive and differentiate into macrophages (or dendritic cells — slides).
    • Macrophages (“big eaters”): eat more bacteria than neutrophils without dying because they digest and exocytose the waste; eventually also clean up pus. Slides: they also present antigens and secrete signaling compounds.

  12. What are the steps in phagocytosis? What is happening at each step?

      1. Recognition/adherence: Toll-like receptors on the macrophage bind common foreign molecules (e.g., the sugar mannose, LPS) — still nonspecific. (Slides start with chemotaxis — chemical signals attract the phagocyte.)
      1. Ingestion: pseudopods wrap around the microbe and join → phagosome (vesicle holding the bacterium).
      1. Digestion: a lysosome (hydrolytic enzymes) fuses with the phagosome → phagolysosome; the bacterium is hydrolyzed into waste.
      1. Elimination: the waste is released by exocytosis.

  13. How do some pathogens avoid phagocytosis? Relate this back to module 19: “How do pathogens avoid host defenses?”

    • Capsule (S. pneumoniae) and M-protein (S. pyogenes) inhibit phagocytosis — the cell is hard to grab.
    • Invasins (Salmonella) hide the pathogen inside host cells.
    • Coagulase (S. aureus) walls the infection off in a clot; kinase (S. pyogenes) dissolves the host’s clot to escape.
    • IgA protease destroys antibodies in mucus (not phagocytosis itself, but another host defense).

  14. What are the four signs and symptoms of inflammation? Why does each occur during an inflammatory response? What protective roles can clotting play in response to cell damage?

    • Redness and heat — vasodilation brings more blood (carrying body heat).
    • Swelling — dilated vessels become leaky, so fluid leaks into the tissue.
    • Pain — the swelling causes pain.
    • Clotting: blood in the area clots to wall in the infection and contain it, forming an abscess; later a scab forms a barrier while tissue heals.

  15. What is pus? An abscess? What is responsible for clearing them away during the healing process?

    • Pus = dead neutrophils, phagocytic waste products, and tissue fluids.
    • Abscess = a walled-off (clotted) collection of the infection and pus at the inflamed site (pimple, boil).
    • Macrophages engulf the pus and debris; the abscess scabs over and heals — small ones leave no trace; large ones (boils) may fill in with scar tissue.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Immunity / susceptibility — Immunity = exemption/freedom from disease, the ability to ward it off; susceptibility = lack of resistance (slides).
  • Lacrimal apparatus — Lacrimal glands make tears → blinking spreads them → lacrimal canals → nasolacrimal duct → nose/throat (swallowed). Tears contain lysozyme.
  • Toll-like receptors — Phagocyte receptors that recognize broad foreign molecules (mannose, LPS) so phagocytes can bind microbes.
  • Introduced, described later — Cytokines, fever, complement, natural killer cells (Module 21); antibody- and cell-mediated immunity (Modules 22–23).
