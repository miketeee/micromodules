Module 23: Specific Defenses — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. How does T-cell independent B-cell activation differ from T-cell dependent B-cell activation?

    • T-independent: a polymerized (repeating) antigen binds many B cell receptors at once, giving a signal strong enough to select the B cell on its own — no T cells needed. Slides: typical of non-protein antigens (polysaccharides, lipids).
    • T-dependent: the B cell binds the antigen but is not activated until a helper T cell confirms the antigen and releases interleukin. Slides: needed for protein antigens.

  2. What are helper T cells? T cell receptors?

    • T cells mature in the thymus. Helper T cells (CD4+) help activate B cells (and macrophages) by recognizing presented antigens and secreting interleukins.
    • T cell receptor: a surface recognition protein with one antigen-binding site (lock and key) — not an antibody and never secreted.

  3. What occurs during T-cell dependent B-cell activation?

      1. A phagocyte (macrophage or dendritic cell) engulfs a pathogen and displays antigen fragments on MHC-2 → it is now an antigen-presenting cell (APC).
      1. The matching helper T cell binds: T cell receptor → antigen and CD4 → MHC-2 (double binding).
      1. The APC secretes interleukin → the helper T cell is activated and clones itself.
      1. The helper T cell secretes interleukin to attract B cells.
      1. A matching B cell binds the antigen but isn’t activated; it displays the antigen on its own MHC-2.
      1. The helper T cell binds that display (TCR + CD4) and secretes interleukin → the B cell is activated → clonal expansion into plasma and memory cells.

  4. What do antigen presenting cells do?

    • They engulf/digest pathogens and display antigen fragments on MHC-2 so helper T cells can inspect them and confirm they are foreign; they also secrete interleukin to activate the helper T cell.

  5. What kinds of cells act as antigen presenting cells (APCs)?

    • Macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells (all display antigen with MHC-2).

  6. What roles are played by MHC-2 and CD4?

    • MHC-2 = the “display stand” used by immune cells (APCs, B cells) to present antigen fragments.
    • CD4 on helper T cells binds MHC-2, while the T cell receptor binds the antigen — a double-binding check.

  7. What kinds of cells have surface MHC-2?

    • Immune cells that present antigens: macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells.

  8. What kinds of cells have surface CD4?

    • Helper T cells (CD4+ cells).

  9. How do interleukins contribute to specific defense?

    • They are cytokine signals between white blood cells: the APC’s interleukin activates the helper T cell; the helper T cell’s interleukin attracts B cells and then activates the selected B cell. (Some interleukins are also pyrogens — Module 21.)

  10. Why are multi-cell interactions and T cell double-binding necessary during adaptive immune responses?

    • They act as multiple confirmations that an antigen is truly foreign before launching a response — the phagocyte, helper T cell, and B cell must all agree.
    • Double binding (TCR + CD4/MHC-2 or TCR + CD8/MHC-1) makes sure each T cell talks only to the right kind of cell: cytotoxic T cells can’t kill immune cells that display antigens on MHC-2. When this recognition system fails, autoimmune diseases can arise.

  11. What are cytotoxic T cells? What do they do?

    • CD8+ T cells (“killer T cells”) that destroy your own cells that have gone bad — virus-infected cells, cancer cells, and foreign cells such as transplanted tissue (slides).
    • They bind a foreign antigen displayed on MHC-1 and secrete perforin and granzyme, inducing apoptosis — sacrificing the cell before it releases new viruses.

  12. What roles are played by MHC-1 and CD8?

    • MHC-1 = the display stand used by regular body cells to show what they’re making (e.g., viral antigens) — a marker of self.
    • CD8 on cytotoxic T cells binds MHC-1 (it can’t bind MHC-2) while the TCR binds the antigen.

  13. What kinds of cells have surface MHC-1?

    • All nucleated body cells (slides) — regular host cells like skin, liver, heart, and intestinal cells.

  14. What kinds of cells have surface CD8?

    • Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+ cells).

  15. What are perforin and granzymes? What do they do? What cells make them?

    • Perforin perforates (punches holes in) the target cell’s membrane; granzymes enter through the holes, go to the nucleus, and trigger apoptosis (slides: they destroy the cell’s proteins).
    • Made by cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells.

  16. What is apoptosis? What role does it play in defense against disease?

    • Pre-programmed cell death: the genome is degraded, the membrane bulges (blebbing), and the cell signals macrophages to clean up its debris.
    • Because the mess is cleaned up quickly, there is no inflammatory response — unlike necrosis (messy death from injury/infection that triggers inflammation).
    • In defense, it destroys infected or cancerous cells before they release viruses or keep dividing.

  17. How do the roles of MHC-1 and MHC-2 differ?

    • MHC-1: on all nucleated body cells; shows antigens from inside the cell (e.g., viral) to cytotoxic T cells (CD8) → can lead to the cell’s destruction.
    • MHC-2: on immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells); presents antigens from engulfed pathogens to helper T cells (CD4) → leads to activation, not destruction.

  18. What are two things Natural Killer cells (NK cells) do? How?

    • Destroy parasites (and other antibody-coated cells): Fc receptors bind antibodies’ constant region on the target → NK cells release lytic chemicals (antibody-dependent cytotoxicity).
    • Destroy damaged host cells that lack MHC-1 — e.g., cancer cells that stop making MHC-1. They kill with perforin and granzymes.

  19. What types of receptors are located on NK cells and how do they work?

    • Fc receptor: binds the constant (Fc) region of antibodies stuck to a target — NK cells recognize antigens indirectly (“specific-ish”).
    • MHC-1 receptor: binding MHC-1 inhibits the NK cell (it’s a healthy self cell); a cell missing MHC-1 is attacked.

  20. Which white blood cells are HIV’s main host cells? What does this mean for HIV infected individuals?

    • Helper T cells (CD4+).
    • HIV destroys the very cells that activate B cells and coordinate defenses → low WBC count and weakened immunity (AIDS), leaving patients vulnerable to other infections.

  21. What are monoclonal antibodies? How are they produced?

    • Large quantities of identical antibodies with the exact same binding site.
    • Inject an antigen into an animal (mouse) → isolate a B cell making the desired antibody → fuse it with an immortal cancer (myeloma) cell using ethylene glycol → a hybridoma that divides indefinitely and keeps secreting the antibody (“golden goose”).
    • Developed in the late 1970s by Georges Köhler (with César Milstein and Niels Jerne, per the module slides).

  22. How are monoclonal antibodies used medically?

    • Diagnostic tests: rapid antigen tests (COVID), pregnancy tests, rapid strep tests. In a COVID strip: dyed monoclonals bind antigen → antigen–antibody–antibody “sandwich” at the test (T) line; chicken antibodies bind rabbit antibodies at the control (C) line. No C line = invalid.
    • Treatments: cancer “magic bullets” (antibodies carrying chemotherapy straight to tumor cells); COVID monoclonals (e.g., Regeneron) that neutralize the spike protein so it can’t bind ACE2.
    • Monoclonal treatment is passive immunity — temporary and doesn’t create memory, unlike a vaccine.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Dendritic cells — Phagocytes with long branch-like extensions that process and present antigens (APCs).
  • Necrosis — Messy cell death from injury or infection that triggers inflammation (vs apoptosis).
  • White blood cell count — Centrifuged blood: plasma on top, RBCs at bottom, WBCs in the middle ‘buffy coat.’ High count usually = infection; low count can mean infection, autoimmune disease, drugs, or AIDS. A differential count identifies each WBC type under a microscope.
  • Cancer & autoimmune disease — Cancer cells may lose MHC-1 (NK cell targets); autoimmune diseases can arise when the CD4/CD8–MHC recognition system fails.
