Module 11: Eukaryotes — Helminths — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What are some distinguishing characteristics of helminths?
    •  Multicellular heterotrophs; range from microscopic to macroscopic.
    •  Have a cuticle (covering) made of collagen and chitin.
    •  Most are harmless, but some parasitic species cause disease.
    •  Three major subgroups: Cestodes, Trematodes, and Nematodes.

  2. What are Cestodes?
    •  Tapeworms. Long, flat bodies (high surface area); attach to the host using a scolex; made of many identical segments called proglottids, each hermaphroditic (containing both ovaries and testes). Example: Taenia.

  3. What is a scolex?
    •  The attachment structure (often with hooks/suckers) at the head end of a tapeworm, used to attach to the host.

  4. What are proglottids?
    •  Repeating, identical segments making up a tapeworm's body; each is hermaphroditic, containing both ovaries and testes.

  5. What are Trematodes? How are they different from Cestodes?
    •  Flukes. Flat bodies (high surface area), complex body systems (oral sucker, intestine, testes, etc.); many are fluid feeders; most are host- and organ-specific (e.g., the liver fluke, Clonorchis).
    •  Different from Cestodes: not made of repeating proglottids and do not attach via a scolex.

  6. What are Nematodes? How are they different from Cestodes and Trematodes?
    •  Roundworms. Complex body systems; most are harmless, but some cause common worldwide disease.
    •  Different: round (not flat) bodies, and not segmented (unlike Cestodes/Trematodes).

  7. What are anticoagulants?
    •  Substances that prevent blood from clotting.

  8. Why do some helminths make anticoagulants?
    •  Blood-feeding helminths produce anticoagulants in their saliva to keep the host's blood flowing freely while they feed.

  9. What types of diseases are helminths a useful treatment for?
    •  Parasitic worms suppress the host's inflammatory response, so they're being researched (helminthic therapy) as a treatment for allergies, Crohn's disease, and Irritable Bowel Syndrome.

  10. How does the antihelminthic drug mebendazole (Vermox) work?
    •  It inhibits sugar absorption in the worm.

  11. What is Caenorhabditis elegans? How is this organism used in science?
    •  A small, free-living nematode used as a model research organism.
    •  Has exactly 959 cells in the adult (a trait called eutely), undergoes 131 programmed cell deaths during development, and has 302 interconnected neurons — valuable for studying cell lineage, development, and the nervous system.

  12. What are arthropods, and how can they contribute to the spread of disease?
    •  Invertebrate animals (e.g., insects, ticks). Many don't cause disease directly but can serve as vectors that transmit disease-causing pathogens to hosts.

  13. What are vectors? How do biological vectors differ from mechanical vectors?
    •  Organisms (typically arthropods) that transmit disease.
    •  Mechanical vectors — transmit infections by carrying pathogens on their body parts (e.g., a house fly).
    •  Biological vectors — transmit infections with the pathogen actually reproducing inside the vector (e.g., a mosquito transmitting malaria, or a tick transmitting Lyme disease).

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Flatworm / Roundworm — general terms for Cestodes/Trematodes (flatworms) vs. Nematodes (roundworms).
  • Tapeworm, Heartworm, Hookworm, Pinworm, Fluke — common names for specific helminth types (see Microbe Spotlight below).
  • Cuticle, Collagen, Chitin — see Q1.
  • Helminthic therapy / Antihelminthic therapy — see Q9 (therapeutic use of worms) vs. drug treatment to kill worms, respectively.
  • Vermicide — a substance that kills worms.
  • Arthropod, Mechanical Vector, Biological Vector — see Q12–13.

Microbe Spotlight - Taenia — Cestode (tapeworm). - Clonorchis — Trematode; the liver fluke. - Ascaris — Nematode (intestinal roundworm). Life cycle: eggs ingested → hatch in the digestive tract → larvae enter blood vessels → travel to the lungs → carried up the trachea and swallowed → adults grow/reproduce in the intestines. - Enterobius — Nematode; the pinworm. - Dirofilaria — Nematode; causes heartworm infections. - Dracunculus — Nematode; the Guinea worm. - Caenorhabditis — free-living model-organism nematode (C. elegans; see Q11).

Disease Spotlight - Ascariasis (Ascaris), Clonorchiasis (Clonorchis), Enterobiasis (Enterobius), Guinea-worm disease/Dracunculiasis (Dracunculus), Heartworm infections/Dirofilariasis (Dirofilaria), Tapeworm infections (Cestodes).

Scientist Spotlight - David Pritchard — studied how hookworm infection can suppress allergies; injected himself with 50 hookworms as part of his research; found that 10 hookworms was the "magic number" for a beneficial effect.

Antibiotic (Antihelminthic) Spotlight - Mebendazole (Vermox) — inhibits sugar absorption (see Q10). - Albendazole (Albenza), Pyrantel (Pin X), Ivermectin — named in this module; their specific target helminth groups are flagged as not covered in the provided sources (see Q14–16).
