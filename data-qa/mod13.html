Module 13: Bacterial Genetics — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What does the Central Dogma of Molecular Genetics state?

    • Genetic information flows DNA → RNA → protein.
    • A gene (DNA) is transcribed into messenger RNA, which travels to the ribosome and is translated into a protein (the correct sequence of amino acids, which then folds on its own).

  2. What is transcription?

    • The process by which a double-stranded DNA gene is copied (transcribed) into a single-stranded RNA (mRNA) molecule — the first stage of gene expression.
    • Only the gene region is copied, not the whole chromosome. The product is an mRNA transcript.

  3. What are the three stages of transcription and what happens during each?

    • Initiation — RNA polymerase binds the promoter, begins unzipping the DNA, and starts RNA synthesis.
    • Elongation — RNA polymerase slides along the gene reading the template strand, adding complementary RNA nucleotides (U opposite A); the DNA re-zips behind it.
    • Termination — RNA polymerase reaches the terminator sequence, falls off, and the finished mRNA is released.

  4. What is RNA polymerase? What does it do during transcription?

    • The enzyme that carries out transcription: it binds the promoter, unzips/unwinds the DNA, and synthesizes a complementary mRNA using the template strand.

  5. Where are promoter and terminator sequences found?

    • Promoter — at the beginning of a gene (“upstream”).
    • Terminator — at the end of a gene (“downstream”).

  6. What role do promoter and terminator sequences play during transcription?

    • Promoter signals where the gene begins; RNA polymerase is strongly attracted to (binds) it. Slides: A/T-rich.
    • Terminator signals where the gene ends; RNA polymerase can’t bind there and falls off, ending transcription. Slides: C/G-rich.

  7. What is translation?

    • The process of translating the mRNA nucleotide sequence into an amino-acid sequence (a polypeptide/protein) at the ribosome — the second stage of gene expression (also called protein synthesis).
    • Major players: mRNA, ribosomes, tRNA, amino acids.

  8. What are the three stages of translation and what happens during each?

    • Initiation — ribosomal subunits assemble on the mRNA at the start codon (AUG); the first tRNA (anticodon UAC) carrying methionine pairs with it.
    • Elongation — the ribosome reads the next codon; the matching tRNA brings its amino acid; a peptide bond forms (slides: requires ATP); the ribosome moves down the mRNA, the empty tRNA leaves, and the chain grows.
    • Termination — the ribosome reaches a stop codon; no tRNA matches it, so the ribosome lets go, the components separate, and the polypeptide is released to fold. (The mRNA is then destroyed so the protein isn’t made nonstop.)

  9. What are codons?

    • Groups of three consecutive mRNA nucleotides that specify a particular amino acid (or start/stop).
    • 4 bases taken 3 at a time = 64 codons for only 20 amino acids, so most amino acids have several codons (redundancy). The genetic code is essentially universal.

  10. What is the most commonly used start codon? (identify its nucleotide sequence)

    • AUG — it also codes for methionine (Met).

  11. What is a stop codon?

    • An mRNA codon that does not code for an amino acid and has no complementary tRNA; it signals the end of translation. (UAA, UAG, UGA — slides say no need to memorize.)

  12. What roles do start and stop codons play during translation?

    • Start codon (AUG): marks where translation begins, where the ribosome binds, and sets the reading frame (everything after is read in triplets).
    • Stop codon: terminates translation — the ribosome releases the mRNA and the finished polypeptide.

  13. What is an anti-codon and where are anti-codons found?

    • A set of three nucleotides on a tRNA that is complementary to an mRNA codon.
    • Found at one end (the bottom loop) of each tRNA; the anticodon determines which amino acid that tRNA carries (e.g., anticodon AAG ↔︎ phenylalanine).

  14. How do mRNA, ribosomes, and tRNA work together during translation?

    • mRNA carries the gene’s message as a series of codons.
    • The ribosome (rRNA + proteins) binds the mRNA, reads the codons, and forms peptide bonds between amino acids.
    • Each tRNA carries a specific amino acid; its anticodon base-pairs with the matching codon, delivering the right amino acid in the right order. tRNAs are recycled (recharged with a new amino acid).

  15. What is an operon?

    • A functional genetic unit consisting of genes and regulatory sequences that control the expression of one or more genes.
    • Parts: regulatory (repressor) gene, promoter, operator, and the structural genes.

  16. Who discovered operons?

    • Jacques Monod — operon model of gene regulation (Nobel Prize 1965).

  17. What is a repressible operon and how does it work?

    • Default state = ON, but it can be turned OFF when needed (analogy: a refrigerator). Example: trp operon.
    • Mechanism (instructor slides): the repressor protein is made inactive (wrong shape), so RNA polymerase transcribes the genes. When a corepressor binds and activates the repressor, it binds the operator and blocks transcription → OFF.

  18. What is an inducible operon and how does it work?

    • Default state = OFF, but it can be turned ON when needed (analogy: a lamp). Example: lac operon.
    • The regulatory gene constantly makes an active repressor protein that binds the operator, blocking RNA polymerase from the promoter → genes OFF.
    • When an inducer is present, it binds the repressor and changes its shape → repressor becomes inactive and falls off → RNA polymerase transcribes the genes → ON.

  19. What is the purpose of the Lac operon? What type of operon is the Lac operon?

    • It controls the lactase genes (lacZ, lacY, lacA) that encode enzymes to break down lactose, so they’re made only when lactose is present.
    • It is an inducible operon; lactose is the inducer. Once the lactose is used up, the repressor returns to its active shape, rebinds the operator, and the genes shut off — a self-regulating negative feedback loop.

  20. What is a mutation?

    • A change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA.
    • Caused by mutagens — chemicals, radiation, or viruses (induced) — or by random replication errors (spontaneous, slides).
    • Usually harmful, occasionally beneficial (raw material for evolution); permanent and heritable.

  21. What are point mutations?

    • Mutations that change a single nucleotide — substitution, insertion, or deletion.

  22. What are substitution point mutations?

    • One nucleotide is replaced by a different nucleotide (e.g., A replaces G). Only one codon is affected, so they are often harmless.

  23. What are the 3 types of substitution point mutations and their effect(s) on cells?

    • From instructor slides (the video shows only a missense example):
    • Silent — no change in the amino acid (the new codon codes for the same one) → no effect.
    • Missense — the amino acid changes to a different amino acid → one wrong amino acid; often tolerable, sometimes harmful.
    • Nonsense — the codon becomes a premature stop codon → protein is cut short, usually nonfunctional.

  24. What are frame-shift errors (frameshift mutations) and their effect(s) on cells?

    • Caused by insertion or deletion of nucleotide(s), which shifts the reading frame — every codon after the mutation is wrong.
    • Produces a protein nothing like the intended one; if it’s an important protein, the cell may die. (Scantron analogy: skip one question and every answer after it is marked wrong.)

  25. How does UV radiation mutate DNA?

    • UV causes thymine dimers — two adjacent thymines covalently bond to each other, kinking the DNA.
    • When transcribed, these cause frameshift errors; longer exposure → more dimers → eventually an essential gene is hit and the cell dies.
    • Basis of UV sterilization (254 nm is most germicidal; also kills endospores) and of skin cancer in humans.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Gene expression — Transcription + translation (slides).
  • mRNA / tRNA / rRNA — mRNA carries the transcribed message (codons); tRNA carries an amino acid and an anticodon; rRNA + proteins form the ribosome, the site of translation.
  • Ribosome — Site of translation; reads codons and links amino acids with peptide bonds.
  • Reading frame — How the mRNA sequence is divided into codons; set by the start codon (slides).
  • Operator / Repressor gene / Repressor protein — Operator = DNA site the repressor binds to block transcription; repressor gene encodes the repressor protein (Q15–18).
  • Mutagens — Chemicals, radiation, and viruses that cause mutations (Q20).
  • Frameshift insertion / deletion — See Q24.
  • Thymine dimer — See Q25.
  • Scientist Spotlight — Jacques Monod — Operon model (Q16).
