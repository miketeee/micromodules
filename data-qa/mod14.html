Module 14: Bacterial Genetics — Part 3 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is meant by horizontal gene transfer?

    • Genetic information passed within a generation — between neighboring cells — rather than from parent to offspring.
    • Contrast: vertical gene transfer = genetic information passed to the next generation (parents → child).
    • Horizontal transfer is what makes genetic engineering possible.

  2. Which living things can transfer genes horizontally?

    • Bacteria (prokaryotes); eukaryotes rely on vertical gene transfer (lecture).
    • Textbook (Ch. 11): HGT is most prevalent in prokaryotes, occurring by transformation, transduction, and conjugation.

  3. What are plasmids and what role do plasmids play in horizontal gene transfer?

    • A small loop of DNA separate from the chromosome that carries accessory (“bonus”) genes.
    • Bacteria exchange copies of plasmids with neighboring cells — the main vehicle of horizontal gene transfer.
    • Scientists can remove plasmids, insert a gene of interest, and get bacteria to take them back up.

  4. How do restriction enzymes work? What are sticky ends?

    • “DNA scissors” — enzymes naturally found in bacteria that cut (cleave) double-stranded DNA at a specific recognition sequence (slides: palindromic). Example: EcoRI cuts at GAATTC.
    • They make a staggered (zigzag) cut, leaving short single-stranded overhangs = sticky ends, which readily base-pair with complementary sticky ends made by the same enzyme.
    • Discovered by Arber, Nathans, and Smith (Nobel 1978).

  5. Who created the first recombinant DNA molecule?

    • Paul Berg (Stanford, 1970; Nobel 1980) — combined DNA from SV40 virus and lambda phage.
    • He refused to put it into E. coli (too risky — SV40 causes cancer in monkeys and E. coli lives in the human gut).

  6. What role do restriction enzymes and ligase play in creating recombinant DNA?

    • Restriction enzymes cut both DNA sources (using the same enzyme so the sticky ends match).
    • DNA ligase (“DNA glue” — the same enzyme that joins Okazaki fragments) seals the pieces together into one recombinant molecule.

  7. What is the goal of traditional genetic engineering? How is this helpful?

    • To put a gene of interest into an organism (e.g., into a plasmid taken up by bacteria) so it expresses that protein — creating new products and GMOs.
    • Helpful because bacteria can then mass-produce useful proteins cheaply (e.g., human insulin) and support research.
    • Cohen & Boyer (1973) made the first recombinant organism.

  8. What was the first FDA-approved genetically engineered medication?

    • Humulin — human insulin made by genetically engineered E. coli (Boyer & Swanson; company Genentech).
    • They built a synthetic insulin gene by working the central dogma backward from insulin’s amino-acid sequence. Replaced pig insulin for type 1 diabetics.
    • Note: the lecture presents Humulin as the landmark product; the “first FDA-approved” wording isn’t stated verbatim in the sources.

  9. What is PCR?

    • Polymerase Chain Reaction — a technique that makes millions to billions of copies of a specific region of DNA in a test tube within a few hours (DNA amplification).
    • Uses template DNA, primers, spare nucleotides, a heat-stable DNA polymerase, and repeated temperature cycles. Copies increase exponentially.

  10. Who developed and first used PCR technology?

    • Kary Mullis (mid-1980s; idea came to him while driving on a date; Nobel Prize 1993).

  11. What role do primers play in PCR?

    • Short synthetic DNA pieces of known sequence that bind the sequences flanking the target region, marking where amplification begins.
    • DNA polymerase can’t start from scratch — it can only extend a primer. Primers decide which region is copied (and, in diagnostics, what is detected).

  12. What is Taq polymerase?

    • A thermostable DNA polymerase isolated from Thermus aquaticus, a thermophilic bacterium from Yellowstone hot springs.
    • It survives PCR’s near-boiling denaturation temperatures without denaturing.

  13. What happens during the three major steps of a PCR cycle?

    • 1. Denaturation (~94–96 °C) — heat breaks hydrogen bonds; the DNA strands separate.
    • 2. Annealing / Priming (~55 °C) — cooled so primers bind their complementary sequences.
    • 3. Extension / Synthesis (~72 °C) — Taq polymerase extends the primers, building new complementary strands.
    • Repeat 25–35×; copies of the target region double each cycle.

  14. What is a thermal cycler (thermocycler)?

    • A machine (programmable hot plate) that automates the repeated temperature changes of PCR cycles. (Mullis originally moved tubes between water baths by hand.)

  15. How is PCR used to diagnose disease?

    • Design primers specific to a pathogen’s unique DNA/RNA sequence, then run PCR on the patient’s sample.
    • Pathogen present → primers bind → amplification → positive result. Pathogen absent → nothing for primers to bind → no amplification → negative.
    • Example: COVID PCR test (RT-PCR). PCR finds the “needle in a haystack.” Also used for genetic-marker tests (Ancestry/23andMe) and forensics.

  16. What is CRISPR?

    • Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats — naturally an adaptive immune system in bacteria (and archaea): they store bits of phage DNA (spacers) and use them to cut up invading viral DNA.
    • Scientists have harnessed it as a programmable gene-editing tool (Cas9 + guide RNA).

  17. Which two scientists discovered and first used CRISPR technology?

    • Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier — Nobel Prize 2020.

  18. What is Cas9? What is guide RNA? Why is guide RNA important?

    • Cas9 — an enzyme that cuts DNA (like a restriction enzyme, but programmable).
    • Guide RNA — a piece of RNA inside Cas9 whose ~20-nucleotide target sequence base-pairs with matching DNA.
    • Guide RNA tells Cas9 where to cut. 20 letters is long enough to be unique in the genome (precise), and the guide can be swapped to target any gene — that’s what makes CRISPR programmable.

  19. How does CRISPR gene editing work?

    • Design a guide RNA matching the target gene → Cas9 + guide RNA scan the DNA, bind the target by complementary base pairing, and cut it.
    • The cell repairs the break one of two ways:
    • Non-homologous end joining — random nucleotides glued in → mutation → gene knocked out (inactivated).
    • Homology-directed repair — scientists supply donor DNA whose ends match the cut; the cell copies it in → new sequence knocked in.

  20. What are some current applications of CRISPR technology?

    • Treating disease: correcting the cystic fibrosis gene in cells in a dish (Clevers, 2015); editing T cells to attack lung cancer (Lu You, 2016); sickle cell disease (Victoria Gray, 2019 — first FDA-approved CRISPR treatment).
    • Organ transplants: pig–human chimeras (Jun Wu); pig kidney with antigen genes knocked out transplanted into Rick Slayman (2024).
    • Food and livestock: longer-lasting white button mushrooms (first approved CRISPR food); edited cows, pigs, chickens.
    • Research: animal models of disease (e.g., edited monkeys). Many targets: blindness, cancer, CF, deafness, diabetes, HIV, muscular dystrophy, sickle cell.

  21. What is germline gene editing? When is it allowed in the United States?

    • Editing the DNA of sperm, eggs, or embryos, so the change is in every cell and is passed on to future generations (no consent from the embryo).
    • The National Academy of Sciences (2017) supports gene-editing research on human embryos and germ cells, but edited embryos may not be implanted — no CRISPR babies (a worldwide moratorium).
    • He Jiankui (China) broke this in 2018 — twin girls Lulu and Nana, CCR5 gene inactivated for HIV resistance; served 3 years in prison.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Vertical gene transfer — Genetic information passed to the next generation (Q1).
  • Conjugation — Not covered in this lecture; textbook (Ch. 11): direct transfer of DNA (often plasmids) between bacteria through a conjugation pilus — one of the three HGT mechanisms.
  • Recombinant DNA — DNA combined from two different sources (Q5–6).
  • DNA cloning — Slides: inserting a gene into a plasmid, introducing it into bacteria, and growing them to make many copies of the gene/protein.
  • Reverse transcriptase — Not covered in this lecture. Textbook (Ch. 12): a retroviral enzyme that makes DNA (cDNA) from an RNA template; used in RT-PCR (e.g., the COVID test). Covered in Module 17.
  • Bioethics — Concerns raised by CRISPR: embryo consent, edits entering the gene pool, unequal access due to cost, creating disease models in primates (monkeys given sleep disorders, anxiety, depression).
  • Microbe Spotlight — Thermus aquaticus — Thermophile from Yellowstone hot springs; source of Taq polymerase (Q12).
  • Non-infectious Disease Spotlight — Cystic fibrosis — Genetic disease: both copies of the CFTR gene, which encodes an ion-transport protein in respiratory cells, are faulty. Hard to treat with CRISPR in adults (every cell carries the mutation); easiest to fix at the embryo stage.
  • Scientist Spotlight — Paul Berg (Q5), Kary Mullis (Q10), Jennifer Doudna & Emmanuelle Charpentier (Q17).
