Module 15: Viruses — Part 1 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. Who discovered viruses? How?

    • Dmitri Ivanovsky (1892), while hunting for the bacterial cause of tobacco mosaic disease.
    • He ground infected plants, passed the material through a membrane filter (which should trap bacteria), and cultured everything trapped — none of the bacterial cultures caused the disease.
    • When he poured the filtrate (what passed through) onto a healthy plant, the plant got the disease → a “filterable infectious poison” smaller than cells. The word virus wasn’t used until the 1930s.

  2. Who was the first to purify and crystallize viruses?

    • Wendell Stanley (1935) — lecture: the first to see viruses with the electron microscope (tobacco mosaic virus, which looked like tiny sticks).
    • Textbook (Ch. 6): Stanley was the first to crystallize TMV and showed it is made of RNA and protein.

  3. What is the size range of most viruses?

    • 20–1,000 nanometers (nm) — much smaller than bacteria; an electron microscope is needed to see them. (TMV ≈ 250 × 18 nm; E. coli ≈ 3,000 × 1,000 nm.)

  4. What are the basic components of a virus? (i.e., which macromolecules?)

    • Nucleic acid (the genome — DNA or RNA, never both) inside a protein coat (capsid).
    • Some also have a lipid envelope (phospholipid bilayer) with protein spikes.

  5. What are capsids?

    • The protein coat (container) that surrounds and protects the viral nucleic acid.

  6. What are capsomeres?

    • The individual protein subunits that assemble to form the capsid (“-mere” = subunit).

  7. How are helical, polyhedral, and complex viruses different from each other? Provide an example for each.

    • Helical — capsomeres wind into a tube (toilet-paper tube → paper-towel tube → garden hose). Ex: tobacco mosaic virus, Ebola (very long).
    • Polyhedral (also called icosahedral) — capsomeres form a many-faceted, diamond-like geometric shape. Ex: human papillomavirus (HPV).
    • Complex — a combination: polyhedral head + helical sheath + tail fibers (“landing gear”). Ex: bacteriophage.

  8. How do non-enveloped and enveloped viruses differ? Provide an example for each.

    • Non-enveloped (naked) — just capsid + nucleic acid, no outer layer. Ex: HPV.
    • Enveloped — capsid surrounded by an envelope, a phospholipid bilayer (membrane-like, but not called a membrane) studded with spike proteins. Ex: influenza virus, SARS-CoV-2.

  9. What are viral spikes and what is their function?

    • Proteins protruding from the viral surface (envelope).
    • They attach the virus to receptors on host cells, determining which cells it can infect. Ex: influenza’s hemagglutinin & neuraminidase; SARS-CoV-2’s spike protein (the main vaccine target).

  10. What types of genomes can viruses have? Be sure to define each.

    • Type of nucleic acid: DNA or RNA.
    • Strandedness: dsDNA (double-stranded DNA — e.g., HPV), ssDNA (single-stranded DNA), dsRNA (double-stranded RNA — e.g., rotavirus), ssRNA (single-stranded RNA, + or − sense — e.g., coronavirus is +ssRNA).
    • Format: linear (free ends) or circular (closed loop).
    • Structure: simple (one piece of nucleic acid) or segmented (multiple pieces — e.g., influenza).

  11. What are bacteriophages?

    • Viruses that infect only bacteria (“bacteria eaters”); complex shape with capsid, sheath, and tail fibers.

  12. What are the steps in the lytic cycle and what occurs during each step?

    • 1. Attachment (adsorption) — phage lands and its proteins bind molecules on the host cell surface.
    • 2. Penetration (entry) — phage injects its DNA through the cell wall (helped by lysozyme) and membrane like a spring-loaded syringe; the capsid/sheath stays outside as an empty husk.
    • 3. Biosynthesis — the host cell copies the phage genome and makes phage proteins (capsids, sheaths, tail fibers) following the viral DNA.
    • 4. Maturation (assembly) — components are assembled into new virions.
    • 5. Release (lysis) — the cell lyses (bursts), releasing ~hundreds (~300) of phages that infect neighboring cells.

  13. What does lysozyme do during a bacteriophage infection?

    • Digests peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall: helps the phage genome get through the wall during penetration, and (made from viral genes) breaks open the cell wall to release new phages.

  14. How are lytic bacteriophage quantified (counted)? What is a plaque?

    • By a plaque assay: serially dilute the phage sample, mix with bacteria that grow as a lawn on a plate, then count plaques and back-calculate the original concentration (like serial-dilution colony counts).
    • Plaque = a clear zone of dead (lysed) bacteria in the lawn — an expanding “ring of death”; each plaque represents one original phage.

  15. What are the steps in the lysogenic cycle?

    • (After attachment and penetration, the phage DNA circularizes.)
    • 1. Integration — phage DNA integrates into the bacterial chromosome by recombination → becomes a prophage.
    • 2. Reproduction — the lysogenic bacterium reproduces normally, copying the prophage into every daughter cell.
    • 3. Induction — the prophage may excise from the chromosome and enter the lytic cycle (doesn’t always occur).

  16. How does the lysogenic cycle relate to the lytic cycle?

    • It’s essentially a delayed lytic cycle: both start with attachment and penetration, then there’s a decision point — go lytic now, or integrate (lysogenic).
    • Lysogeny multiplies the phage’s instructions into many cells; induction later sends the prophage back into the lytic cycle. Not all phages can do lysogeny.

  17. What is a prophage?

    • Phage DNA integrated into the bacterial chromosome — not an actual virus yet, but capable of becoming one (“pro” = before).

  18. What is induction?

    • When the prophage excises (cuts itself out) of the bacterial chromosome and initiates the lytic cycle.

  19. What causes induction to occur during the lysogenic cycle?

    • Stress on the host cell — the prophage senses the host may die and switches to lytic (“if the host is going to die, we might as well make new viruses”).

  20. What is lysogenic conversion? Why is it important medically?

    • A change in the phenotype (appearance, behavior, metabolism) of a bacterium caused by the presence of prophage — the cell expresses some prophage genes.
    • Medically: prophage genes can encode toxins, turning otherwise harmless bacteria into dangerous ones:
    • Streptococcus pyogenes + prophage → scarlet fever toxin; Clostridium botulinum + prophage → botulism toxin.

  21. Who discovered and named bacteriophage? How were they discovered?

    • Félix d’Hérelle (1916).
    • He filtered a bacterial culture in which cells were dying, added the filtrate to fresh bacteria, and saw plaques — the same filtrate method Ivanovsky used.

  22. What is bacteriophage therapy and what infections can it treat?

    • Using bacteriophages to treat bacterial infections.
    • d’Hérelle gave filtered feces from healthy WWI soldiers to soldiers dying of bacterial dysentery — and cured them.
    • Faded after penicillin, now being revived for antibiotic-resistant infections; fecal transplant therapy is used for C. difficile infections.

  23. Why is phage therapy safe for use in humans and animals?

    • Phages infect only bacteria — they can’t infect human/animal cells — and each is highly specific to one type of bacterium.
    • They are self-eliminating: once the target bacteria are gone, the (non-living) phages have no hosts and degrade.

  24. What are pros and cons of phage therapy compared to traditional antibiotics?

    • Antibiotics: non-specific (kill good bacteria too → e.g., yeast infections), require large doses over many days (10–14), and face growing resistance.
    • Phage therapy pros: highly specific (spares good bacteria), small doses (self-amplifying — phages multiply in the target), self-eliminating.
    • Phage therapy con: you must identify the exact bacterium to match the right phage → extra testing/expense.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Virus — A non-living, obligate intracellular parasite — it must get inside a host cell to be reproduced, harming or killing the cell.
  • Nucleocapsid — The capsid together with the nucleic acid it encloses (standard term; not explicitly defined in the lecture).
  • Naked virus — = non-enveloped virus (Q8).
  • Plaque / Plaque assay / Phage therapy / Lysogenic conversion — See Q14, Q20, Q22.
  • Scientist Spotlight — Félix d’Hérelle (Q21); Rosalind Franklin — X-ray crystallography of TMV revealed its helical structure; Dmitri Ivanovsky (Q1); Wendell Stanley (Q2).
  • Virus Spotlight — Ebola — Helical virus whose capsid tube is very long (“garden hose”); among the largest viruses.
  • Virus Spotlight — Human papillomavirus (HPV) — Polyhedral, non-enveloped, dsDNA; causes genital warts/papillomas (and cervical cancer — Module 17).
  • Virus Spotlight — Influenza — Enveloped, helical capsid, segmented RNA genome; spikes = hemagglutinin & neuraminidase.
  • Virus Spotlight — SARS-CoV-2 — Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome coronavirus 2; enveloped, helical capsid, single-stranded (+) RNA genome; spike proteins (vaccine target).
  • Virus Spotlight — Tobacco mosaic virus — Helical RNA plant virus; first virus discovered (Ivanovsky) and first seen with the EM (Stanley).
