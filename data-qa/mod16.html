Module 16: Viruses — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is meant by viral host range?

    • The specific types of host cells (species and/or cell types) a virus can infect. Most viruses have a narrow host range.
    • Ex: HIV infects only human CD4+ helper T cells; cats have FIV and monkeys SIV — each limited to its own host.

  2. What makes virus-host interactions specific?

    • Viral surface proteins (spikes) must bind specific receptor/attachment sites on the host cell — a lock-and-key fit.
    • Ex: HIV binds CD4; SARS-CoV-2’s spike binds the ACE2 receptor.

  3. What occurs during direct penetration? Name a virus using this method.

    • The capsid attaches to host-cell receptors and the viral genome is injected through the membrane into the cell (similar to bacteriophage).
    • Used by a few non-enveloped viruses; the least common entry method. Ex: human enterovirus (meningitis).

  4. What occurs during membrane fusion? Name a virus using this method.

    • The viral envelope fuses (“melts”) into the host cell membrane (both are phospholipid), releasing the capsid into the cytoplasm; uncoating then frees the genome.
    • Used by many enveloped viruses. Ex: SARS-CoV-2 and HIV.

  5. What occurs during entry by endocytosis? Name a virus using this method.

    • The host cell engulfs the virus with pseudopods into a vesicle; the capsid is released from the vesicle and uncoated to free the genome.
    • Used by enveloped and non-enveloped viruses. Ex: influenza virus.

  6. What is uncoating? Which types of viral entry require uncoating?

    • Breaking apart the capsid to release the viral genome inside the cell.
    • Required after membrane fusion and endocytosis (the whole capsid gets in). Not needed for direct penetration, since only the genome enters.

  7. How do acute, latent, and persistent viral infections differ from each other? Provide an example virus for each.

    • Acute — the virus immediately enters a productive phase, making many new viruses quickly (may lyse cells); rapid spike then resolves (similar to the phage lytic cycle). Ex: adenovirus (common cold).
    • Latent — viral genome sits in the nucleus as episomal DNA (not part of the chromosome), dormant for months/years, then becomes productive; can recur. Ex: herpes simplex virus (cold sores); also varicella-zoster (chickenpox → shingles, slides).
    • Persistent — viral genome integrates into the host chromosome as a provirus; new viruses are made at a slow, detectable rate without disease; may later become productive. Ex: HIV (HIV-positive → later AIDS).

  8. How do naked viruses exit host cells? How does this affect the host cell?

    • By lysis — the cell ruptures and the viruses burst out to infect neighbors (like the lytic cycle).
    • The host cell is destroyed.

  9. How do enveloped viruses typically exit host cells?

    • By budding — viral spike proteins are inserted into the host’s plasma membrane, then the capsid pushes out through the membrane, wrapping itself in it (“wolf in sheep’s clothing”).
    • A few viruses budding may let the cell survive; thousands budding at once strip so much membrane that the cell is destroyed.
    • (Exception: SARS-CoV-2 exits by exocytosis — see Q16.)

  10. What is the typical source of the viral envelope?

    • The host cell’s plasma membrane — a patch of host membrane containing viral proteins, acquired during budding.
    • (SARS-CoV-2 instead gets its envelope from rough ER/Golgi membranes inside the cell.)

  11. What role does hemagglutinin play in the viral life cycle?

    • A spike protein used for attachment — binds receptors on host cells so the virus can enter.

  12. What role does neuraminidase play in the viral life cycle?

    • A spike enzyme needed for release (budding): it digests the proteins tethering the budding virus to the cell so it can “lift off” (like cutting a hot-air balloon’s ropes).

  13. Which viruses have hemagglutinin and neuraminidase?

    • Influenza viruses.

  14. What is H1N1?

    • An influenza virus subtype named for its spikes: hemagglutinin type 1 and neuraminidase type 1 (other combos: H1N2, H2N1, etc.).
    • Textbook: H1N1 strains caused the 1918 and 2009 flu pandemics.

  15. How does the antiviral drug Tamiflu work?

    • Tamiflu (oseltamivir) is a neuraminidase inhibitor → prevents successful budding/release of new flu viruses.
    • It doesn’t kill viruses or block entry; slowing release gives the immune system time. Not effective against SARS-CoV-2 (which exits by exocytosis, not neuraminidase-dependent budding).

  16. How does SARS-CoV-2 enter, infect, and exit host cells?

    • Attachment & entry: spike protein binds ACE2 receptors on respiratory cells; the envelope fuses with the cell membrane (membrane fusion) and the ssRNA genome is released.
    • Biosynthesis & assembly: host replicates the viral genome and makes viral proteins; new viruses assemble in the cytoplasm, getting their envelope from rough ER/Golgi membranes.
    • Release: new viruses enter lysosomes, which they de-acidify so they can’t be digested; viruses are released as “waste” by exocytosis — which doesn’t directly harm the cell.
    • Severe COVID symptoms come mainly from an overactive immune response.

  17. How does the antiviral drug remdesivir work?

    • Disrupts the viral polymerase (copying of the viral genome); it resembles a nucleotide and introduces errors/mutations into new genomes.
    • Moderately effective at preventing severe disease/hospitalization, especially if taken early.

  18. What caused the Flu Pandemic of 1918?

    • An influenza virus (textbook: an H1N1 strain). ~500 million infected, ~100 million deaths — more deaths than the Plague (50 million).
    • Unusually deadly to healthy individuals with strong immune systems (severe hypersensitive response). No one had seen a virus yet in 1918.

  19. What did people do to try to stop Flu Pandemic of 1918?

    • State and local mask mandates — masks were found to reduce transmission (with pro- and anti-mask camps, like during COVID). There were no antivirals or knowledge of viral structure.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Pandemic — A worldwide outbreak of disease (e.g., 1918 flu, COVID). Not formally defined in the lecture.
  • Mechanisms of viral exit — Lysis (naked viruses, Q8); budding (enveloped, Q9); exocytosis (SARS-CoV-2, Q16) — slides note exocytosis can apply to both.
  • “Life” cycle of animal viruses — Attachment → Entry (+ uncoating) → Biosynthesis → Assembly → Release. Compare phage: animal viruses can undergo latency instead of lysogeny (slides).
  • Virus Spotlight — Adenovirus — Acute infection; common cold.
  • Virus Spotlight — Herpes simplex virus — Latent infection; cold sores; recurs.
  • Virus Spotlight — HIV — Enveloped retrovirus; enters by membrane fusion; infects CD4+ helper T cells; persistent infection (provirus); exits by budding.
  • Virus Spotlight — Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) — Slides: human herpesvirus 3; causes chickenpox, then stays latent and can reactivate as shingles (pain can last as long as the rash).
  • Disease Spotlight — AIDS (HIV’s productive phase), Chicken pox (VZV), Common cold (adenovirus), COVID (SARS-CoV-2), The Flu (influenza), Shingles (reactivated VZV).
  • Antiviral Drugs Spotlight — Oseltamivir/Tamiflu (Q15); remdesivir (Q17).
