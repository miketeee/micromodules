Module 24: Vaccines and Antimicrobials — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What kind of pathogen caused smallpox?

    • A virus (variola — the root of “variolation”); it killed many millions through history.

  2. What were the Chinese doing by the early 1400’s to prevent smallpox?

    • Taking a scab from a smallpox patient — a “Goldilocks” scab, not too moist or too dry — and having a healthy person inhale it. It didn’t always work but lowered the chance of getting smallpox.

  3. What were Europeans doing by the early 1700’s to prevent smallpox?

    • Variolation: placing smallpox scabs directly beneath the skin through a small cut (parenteral route) — a small dose of virus meant to cause immunity without full disease. Risk: you could give the person smallpox.

  4. How did Jenner develop a smallpox vaccine? How was Jenner’s approach different from earlier approaches?

    • Edward Jenner (variolated himself as a child) noticed milkmaids who caught cowpox rarely got smallpox.
    • He placed cowpox scabs beneath the skin (tested on children) → immunity to smallpox.
    • Difference: he used a related, much milder virus (cowpox) instead of smallpox itself, so it was far safer.
    • Vaccine comes from Latin vacca (cow); the word was coined later by Louis Pasteur, who made other vaccines (famously rabies).

  5. What are five different types of vaccines? How do they differ from one another?

    • Goal of all vaccines: artificially expose you to an antigen so B cells are selected → plasma cells, memory cells, and a titer in advance (artificial active immunity); a real exposure then acts like a secondary one.
    • Attenuated whole agent: whole pathogen, weakened but still able to reproduce a little → mimics infection closely. Ex: MMR, Sabin polio.
    • Inactivated whole agent: whole pathogen that can’t reproduce. Ex: rabies, influenza (flu shot), Salk polio. (You can’t get the flu from the shot; symptoms are your immune response.)
    • Subunit: just one antigen (e.g., a surface protein). Ex: hepatitis B, HPV.
    • Toxoid: a denatured bacterial toxin. Ex: tetanus, diphtheria (most bacterial vaccines are toxoids).
    • Nucleic acid: DNA or mRNA encoding an antigen; your cells make it. Ex: COVID.
    • Viruses for some vaccines (flu) are grown in eggs → you’re asked about egg allergies (cell culture is a costlier alternative).

  6. How do mRNA COVID vaccines work?

    • Lipid nanoparticles (phospholipid bubbles) carrying mRNA for the spike protein are injected → they fuse with host cells (ideally immune cells like dendritic cells/macrophages) and release the mRNA.
    • Ribosomes translate it into spike protein → fragments loaded on MHC-2 → the cell acts as an APC → a matching B cell (with helper T cell help) is selected → plasma cells (anti-spike antibodies that neutralize the virus’s binding to ACE2) and memory cells.
    • mRNA (Pfizer, Moderna): stored ultra-cold (RNA is unstable); mRNA also acts as an adjuvant boosting response via interferon; two doses (~2 weeks apart → higher titer); second dose can bring stronger side effects (cytokines).
    • DNA (Johnson & Johnson, AstraZeneca): room-temperature stable; DNA carried into the nucleus by an inactivated adenoviral vector, transcribed to mRNA; single dose — better for remote areas worldwide.

  7. Who pioneered development of synthetic antimicrobial medications?

    • Paul Ehrlich — fascinated by stains/dyes; saw a dye travel to specific spots in a rabbit and imagined “magic bullets” (a toxic drug attached to a stain that targets a pathogen).
    • Background: Lister’s germicides sterilized surfaces but were far too toxic to put inside the body.

  8. How did Preparation 606 (“Salvarsan”) work?

    • Arsenic attached to a dye — the dye carried the toxic arsenic to the target pathogen (a magic bullet). Hundreds of versions failed before #606 worked.
    • It was the first synthetic antimicrobial drug (first magic bullet). Some patients were allergic and died, which haunted Ehrlich, though it saved far more.

  9. What was Preparation 606 (“Salvarsan”) used to treat?

    • Trypanosomes (protozoa → sleeping sickness) and then syphilis (Treponema pallidum, a spirochete bacterium) — Ehrlich thought they looked similar under the microscope.

  10. Who discovered penicillin? How?

    • Alexander Fleming (inspired by Ehrlich): returning from vacation, he found a staphylococcal plate contaminated with Penicillium mold and a zone of inhibition around it → the mold released a bacteria-killing substance → penicillin.
    • Promising in mice, but he was denied funding. During WWII, Ernst Chain and Howard Florey revived and mass-produced it; all three shared the Nobel Prize (story: The Mold in Dr. Florey’s Coat).

  11. What do the major types of antibiotics target and how do they function?

    • Inhibit nucleic acid synthesis: Cipro / quinolones (block DNA gyrase).
    • Inhibit protein synthesis: tetracycline, streptomycin, erythromycin — target the bacterial 70S ribosome.
    • Inhibit cell wall synthesis: penicillin, cephalosporin, vancomycin (block peptidoglycan cross-linking).
    • Interfere with enzyme function: sulfanilamide, trimethoprim (folic acid pathway).
    • Slides add a fifth target: disrupting the cell membrane (polymyxin B, colistin). Other antimicrobials: Flagyl (protozoan DNA), Monistat (ergosterol), Vermox (worm sugar absorption), Tamiflu (neuraminidase), remdesivir (viral polymerase).

  12. How do narrow spectrum and broad spectrum antibiotics differ?

    • Narrow spectrum: effective against only certain types of bacteria (e.g., mainly Gram-positive). Ex: penicillin, vancomycin.
    • Broad spectrum: effective against many types, by targeting a near-universal process. Ex: sulfanilamide, Cipro.

  13. When should broad spectrum drugs be used? Why?

    • When the pathogen is unknown (e.g., a walk-in clinic patient with a respiratory infection) or sensitivity is unknown — slides: also mixed infections; faster and often cheaper.
    • Downside: they kill good normal flora too, raising the risk of resistance and superinfections (C. diff, yeast infections — slides).

  14. When should narrow spectrum drugs be used? Why?

    • When the pathogen is known — they target the bad guys while sparing the mutualistic normal flora; fewer side effects and lower resistance risk (slides).

  15. What are two new approaches to treating antibiotic-resistant infections?

    • Resistance = evolution by natural selection on a short time scale; new antibiotics aren’t being found fast enough.
    • Phage therapy (pioneered by Félix d’Hérelle): inject bacteriophages that infect the specific pathogen — highly specific (spares good bacteria), small dose (self-amplifying), self-eliminating once the pathogen is gone.
    • Quorum-sensing inhibitors (Bonnie Bassler, Princeton) — block bacterial communication so they never turn toxic.

  16. What is quorum sensing? How does it normally work in bacteria?

    • Bacteria release a signal molecule and have receptors for it to sense how many of their kind are nearby. With enough cells (a quorum), signal binds neighbors’ receptors and they all switch on together.
    • Discovered via Vibrio fischeri on the bobtail squid, which all bioluminesce at once. Pathogens use it to release toxins simultaneously and overwhelm the immune system (a lone toxin-maker would just attract the immune system).

  17. How might future antimicrobial medications be used to block bacterial quorum sensing? Why might this be helpful medically?

    • A signal inhibitor binds the receptors without triggering the response → cells never learn they have a quorum → they never turn toxic, and the immune system can clear them.
    • Helpful because it disarms pathogens without killing them broadly — a new option as antibiotic resistance grows. (Not yet in human trials, per the lecture.)

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Selective toxicity (slides only) — Harming the pathogen but not the patient; highest when the drug targets something only the pathogen has (beta-lactams → peptidoglycan; sulfa → folic acid; Monistat → ergosterol); harder for eukaryotic pathogens like helminths.
  • Antibiotic resistance — Bacteria evolving to survive antibiotics (natural selection).
  • Superinfection (slides) — Broad-spectrum drugs kill normal flora, letting resistant opportunists (C. difficile, Candida) overgrow.
  • Cowpox / smallpox — Mild cow virus that protects against the deadly human smallpox virus.
