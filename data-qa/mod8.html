Module 8: Control of Microbial Growth — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What does sterilization mean within a microbiology context?
    •  The removal or destruction of ALL live microorganisms (and viruses), including endospores.

  2. What is disinfection?
    •  The removal, destruction, or inhibition of growth of MOST (not all) microorganisms.

  3. What are disinfectants and antiseptics? How do they differ?
    •  Disinfectant — a chemical used to remove, destroy, or inhibit growth of most microorganisms.
    •  Antiseptic — a disinfectant safe/suitable for use on living tissue (skin/mucous membranes).
    •  Difference: where it can safely be applied — inanimate surfaces vs. living tissue.

  4. What is sanitization? Provide examples.
    •  Reduction of microorganisms to a level considered safe for public health.
    •  Examples: washing dishes/utensils at a restaurant, cleaning surfaces at a public pool.

  5. What is degerming? Provide examples.
    •  Mechanical removal of microorganisms from a limited area, most often the skin.
    •  Examples: hand washing with soap and water, an alcohol swab before an injection.

  6. What kind of hand soap is recommended for routine at-home hand washing?
    •  Regular (non-antibacterial) soap — liquid soap is more effective than bar soap.
    •  Antibacterial soap isn't necessary at home because its germicidal chemicals need more contact time than a typical (~15-second) hand wash provides.

  7. What makes Bacillus, Clostridium, and Clostridioides bacteria hard to control?*
    •  They form     endospores***, which are highly resistant to heat, drying, and many chemical disinfectants, letting them survive treatments that kill ordinary vegetative cells.

  8. What are critical, semicritical, and noncritical medical items? Provide examples.
    •  Critical items — penetrate tissue, must be sterile (needles, scalpels).
    •  Semi-critical items — contact mucous membranes, need high-level disinfection (endoscopes, specula).
    •  Non-critical items — contact only unbroken skin, need routine cleaning (stethoscopes, exam benches).

  9. How does heat kill microorganisms?
    •  It denatures proteins (including enzymes), destroying their 3D shape and function.

  10. What are 2 examples of dry heat sterilization?
    •  Microincineration (incineration) and dry heat ovens (e.g., 170°C for 3–4 hours); both destroy endospores.

  11. What is an example of moist heat?
    •  Boiling water at 100°C for 10 minutes.

  12. Why is boiling not sufficient for sterilization?
    •  Boiling at 100°C does not destroy endospores, so it disinfects but does not fully sterilize.

  13. How does an autoclave work? Describe the conditions inside an autoclave.
    •  Uses pressure to raise water's boiling point above 100°C — 121°C at 15 PSI for 15 minutes — hot enough to destroy even endospores, achieving true sterilization.

  14. What is pasteurization? What is its purpose? Provide examples.
    •  Rapid heating at 72°C for 15 seconds.
    •  Purpose: reduce microbial load (disinfect, not sterilize) without ruining the product's quality.
    •  Examples: milk, fruit juices, beer.

  15. How does membrane filtration work? Provide examples.
    •  Liquid or air passes through a filter with pores smaller than about 0.4 µm, physically trapping microorganisms without heat.
    •  Useful for heat-sensitive liquids; HEPA filters used for air (e.g., operating rooms).

  16. How does radiation work to control microorganisms?
    •  It mutates DNA, damaging genetic material so organisms can't replicate or function properly.

  17. When is ultraviolet (UV) radiation used within a healthcare setting?
    •  For surface/air disinfection — e.g., operating rooms, and treating food and air. UV wavelengths of 200–400 nm are used; 254 nm is the most germicidal.

  18. What are common examples of preservation? How does each work?
    •  Refrigeration — slows enzyme activity/metabolism, keeping bacteria in lag phase longer.
    •  Freeze drying — sublimates out moisture, removing water bacteria need.
    •  Dehydration — removes water by drying.
    •  Salting / Sugaring — draw water out of cells via osmosis (hypertonic environment), inhibiting growth.

  19. For each germicide: category of microbial control, safety concerns, and a specific example:
    •  Alcohols — Antiseptic; generally safe/inexpensive. Example: 70% isopropyl alcohol.
    •  Aldehydes — mutate DNA; some are carcinogenic. Example: formaldehyde.
    •  Biguanides — Antiseptic; common in mouthwash. Example: chlorhexidine.
    •  Ethylene oxide — pressurized gas; kills endospores; carcinogenic. Used for heat-sensitive items like linens/pillows.
    •  Halogens — fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine; fluorine is most effective but most toxic, iodine is least effective but safest (used as an antiseptic); chlorine used as a disinfectant in pools/hot tubs.
    •  Peroxides — Disinfectant/antiseptic. Example: hydrogen peroxide (H2O2 → H2O + O2, via catalase); works best on inanimate objects.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Sterilant — an agent capable of achieving sterilization.
  • Sanitary — a condition meeting public-health cleanliness standards.
  • Germicide — a general term for any agent that kills germs/microorganisms.
  • Bacteriostatic — describes an agent that inhibits/stops bacterial growth and reproduction without necessarily killing the bacteria (contrast with bactericidal, which kills).

Microbe Spotlight - Bacillus, Clostridium, Clostridioides — endospore-forming genera that are especially hard to sterilize/control (see Q7).
