Module 1: Introduction to Microbiology (Part 1) — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What was The Plague? When did it occur?
    •  Also called the Black Death; occurred 1346–1350.
    •  About one-third of the European population died in this short period.

  2. How did The Plague contribute to the birth of microbiology?
    •  The scale of death pushed people to seek a rational explanation rather than religious ones (God's wrath / Satan's victory).
    •  This search for cause-and-effect launched the Renaissance, generally considered the birth of modern science — and eventually microbiology.

  3. What caused The Plague?
    •  Overcrowding and poor sanitation (sewage in the streets) attracted rats.
    •  Rats carried fleas, and the fleas carried the bacterium Yersinia pestis — the true cause of the Plague.
    •  This was only confirmed within the last few decades via DNA evidence from exhumed victims.

  4. Who was the first to see microorganisms? When?
    •  Anton van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch drapery merchant and expert lens-grinder, in the 1670s.
    •  He observed tiny living things in pond/rain-barrel water and called them "animalcules."

  5. How were bacteria discovered?
    •  Leeuwenhoek examined scrapings from teeth (his own, and later a man with rotting teeth) under his microscope.
    •  He drew what he saw — rod, round, and spiral shapes — which we now recognize as bacteria.

  6. What is the size range of bacteria?
    •  1 to 10 micrometers (µm).

  7. Why did the initial discovery of bacteria not lead to the recognition that microbes cause disease?
    •  Leeuwenhoek noticed more bacteria in rotting teeth than healthy teeth, but he reversed cause and effect: he concluded rotting teeth created the ideal environment for the spontaneous formation of bacteria — not that bacteria caused the rotting.
    •  It took about 200 more years to correct this.

  8. What is the Germ Theory of disease?
    •  The theory that microorganisms cause disease.
    •  Louis Pasteur proposed the idea; Robert Koch performed the rigorous experimental proof, in the mid-to-late 1800s.

  9. What are the three Domains of Life?
    •  Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya — grouped by relatedness of nucleotide sequences.

  10. What are prokaryotes and eukaryotes?
    •  Prokaryotes (Domains Bacteria & Archaea): small (1–10 µm) cells with no nucleus.
    •  Eukaryotes (Domain Eukarya): larger (10–100 µm) cells with a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

  11. How are prokaryotes and eukaryotes similar? How are they different?
    •  Similar: both are living cells with a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material.
    •  Different: prokaryotes are smaller, lack a nucleus/organelles, and are always unicellular; eukaryotes are larger, have a nucleus and organelles, and can be unicellular or multicellular.

  12. How are Bacteria and Archaea similar? Different?
    •  Similar: both prokaryotic, small (1–10 µm), unicellular.
    •  Different: Bacteria have peptidoglycan cell walls, are the most abundant life on Earth, and ~3–5% are pathogenic. Archaea live in extreme environments (heat, salt, acid, pressure) and are not known to cause disease.

  13. What are the four Kingdoms in the Domain Eukarya?
    •  Animalia (multicellular, heterotrophic — ingests food)
    •  Plantae (multicellular, autotrophic/photosynthetic)
    •  Fungi (uni- or multicellular, heterotrophic — decomposes food)
    •  Protista (mostly unicellular; the "miscellaneous" kingdom — protozoa & algae)

  14. How are microorganisms named?
    •  By Binomial Classification — a Latinized Genus + species name (e.g., Streptococcus pyogenes).

  15. What can you learn about microorganisms from their names?
    •  Latin/Greek roots often describe the organism's appearance or effect, e.g.:

    • strepto = chain, staphylo = cluster, coccus = berry
    • pyo = pus, gen = formation, aureus = golden

  16. What are viruses? What are they made of?
    •  Non-living, obligate intracellular parasites.
    •  Made of a protein capsid surrounding a strand of nucleic acid. Examples: HIV, HPV.

  17. What are prions? What are they made of?
    •  Infectious proteins — no genetic material at all.
    •  Example: the prion causing Mad Cow Disease (Bovine Spongiform Encephalitis).

  18. What are viroids? What kinds of organisms do they infect?
    •  Infectious pieces of nucleic acid (RNA).
    •  Only known to infect plants (e.g., Avocado Sunblotch Viroid).

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Cells — the basic unit of life; term coined by Robert Hooke after observing cork under a microscope.
  • Taxonomy — the science of identifying, classifying, and naming biological species.
  • Three Domain System — modern classification system splitting life into Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.
  • Unicellular — composed of a single cell. Multicellular — composed of many cooperating cells.

Microbe Spotlight - Candida albicans — unicellular fungus (yeast); causes vaginitis. - Staphylococcus aureus — cluster-shaped bacteria; causes Staph infections/MRSA. - Streptococcus pyogenes — chain-shaped bacteria; causes strep throat. - Yersinia pestis — bacterium carried by fleas on rats; cause of the Black Plague.

Disease Spotlight - The Black Plague — caused by Yersinia pestis. - Staph Infection – MRSA — caused by Staphylococcus aureus; MRSA is an antibiotic-resistant strain. - Strep Throat — caused by Streptococcus pyogenes. - Vaginitis — caused by the yeast Candida albicans.

Scientist Spotlight - Robert Hooke — pioneered microscopy; published Micrographia (1664); coined the term "cell." - Robert Koch — provided the rigorous experimental proof of the Germ Theory of Disease. - Anton van Leeuwenhoek — first to observe bacteria and other microorganisms ("animalcules"). - Louis Pasteur — proposed that microorganisms could cause disease.
