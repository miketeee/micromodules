Module 4: Prokaryotic Cells — Part 2 — Study Guide Q&A

Study Guide Questions

  1. What is the structure and function of the cell (cytoplasmic) membrane?
    •  A phospholipid bilayer studded with membrane proteins, enclosing the cytoplasm.
    •  It is selectively/differentially permeable — small nonpolar substances pass through easily; large or polar substances need help (transport proteins).

  2. What are phospholipids, and how are they able to form bilayers?
    •  A class of lipids that are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails.
    •  In water, they spontaneously arrange into a bilayer — tails facing inward (away from water), heads facing outward — forming the membrane.

  3. What roles do proteins play in the cell membrane?
    •  Transport (moving substances across), Receptors (bind signaling molecules), Ligands (molecules that bind receptors), and Enzymes (catalyze reactions).

  4. What is a concentration gradient?
    •  An unequal distribution of a solute between two regions — a difference in concentration from one area to another.

  5. What is simple diffusion?
    •  Passive (no energy required) net movement of a solute from higher to lower concentration; used by small, nonpolar molecules (e.g., O2, CO2).

  6. What are solutes and solvents, and how do they relate to one another?
    •  A solute (e.g., salt) is dissolved in a solvent (e.g., water). Concentration = amount of solute ÷ amount of solvent.

  7. What is osmosis?
    •  Passive diffusion of the solvent (water) from an area of higher water concentration to an area of lower water concentration, across a membrane.

  8. What does it mean if the cell's surroundings are isotonic to the cytoplasm?
    •  Equal solute concentration inside and outside the cell; water moves equally in both directions — no net change in cell volume.

  9. What does it mean if the cell's surroundings are hypotonic to the cytoplasm?
    •  Lower solute concentration outside than inside; net water movement is into the cell — can cause the cell to swell and undergo lysis.

  10. What does it mean if the cell's surroundings are hypertonic to the cytoplasm?
    •  Higher solute concentration outside than inside; net water movement is out of the cell — can cause the cell to shrink (plasmolysis).

  11. What is facilitated diffusion?
    •  Passive (no energy required) movement of a substance from higher to lower concentration with the help of a transport protein; used for larger/polar/charged molecules (e.g., glucose, amino acids).

  12. What is active transport, and how does it relate to gradients across the cell membrane?
    •  Movement of a substance from lower to higher concentration (against its gradient). Requires energy and a protein pump.
    •  Often used to create gradients (e.g., a proton pump generating a proton motive force).

  13. What is bulk transport?
    •  Movement of large or numerous materials across the membrane using vesicles: Endocytosis (material moves INTO the cell) and Exocytosis (material moves OUT of the cell).

  14. How are endocytosis and exocytosis similar and different?
    •  Similar: both are bulk transport using membrane-bound vesicles to move large amounts of material.
    •  Different: endocytosis brings material in; exocytosis sends material out.

  15. What is the structure and function of the cytoplasm?
    •  The gelatinous "cell fluid," mostly water, with dissolved nutrients, enzymes, and other biomolecules; the site of many biochemical reactions.

  16. What is the function of ribosomes?
    •  The site of protein synthesis (translation) — they translate mRNA into a chain of amino acids (polypeptide/protein).

  17. How do prokaryotic and eukaryotic ribosomes differ? Why is this clinically relevant?
    •  Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S; eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S (larger).
    •  Clinically relevant because some antibiotics (e.g., tetracycline) can selectively target the 70S prokaryotic ribosome, killing/inhibiting bacteria without harming the patient's 80S ribosomes.

  18. What are inclusions (storage granules), and what is their function?
    •  Precipitated "chunks" of crystallized storage molecules (e.g., glycogen).
    •  Store excess nutrients while minimizing osmotic pressure/water uptake.

  19. What is the nucleoid? What is it made of, and what is its function?
    •  The region of the cytoplasm containing the bacterial chromosome.
    •  Made of a large, double-stranded circular loop of DNA. Function: houses the cell's genetic information (genes).

  20. What are plasmids? What are they made of, and what is their function?
    •  Small, extra loops of DNA (separate from the main chromosome) containing "accessory" genes.
    •  Copies can be exchanged between neighboring cells (via conjugation through the sex pilus).

  21. Why are plasmids clinically relevant?
    •  They often carry antibiotic-resistance genes, and because they can be exchanged between bacteria (conjugation), resistance can spread rapidly.

  22. What are endospores, and which genera of bacteria typically produce them?
    •  A highly resilient "cell within a cell" (dormant structure) formed in response to stressful environmental conditions, encased in a resilient peptidoglycan layer.
    •  Produced by Bacillus, Clostridium, and Clostridioides.

  23. Why are endospores clinically relevant?
    •  They are extremely resistant to heat, desiccation, and disinfectants, letting bacteria survive harsh conditions (including some sterilization attempts) and later germinate back into active, disease-causing cells.

  24. What is C. diff?
    •  Clostridioides difficile — its spores and vegetative cells are ingested; vegetative cells are killed by stomach acid but spores survive and germinate in the small intestine; the bacteria then multiply and cause infection/diarrhea in the large intestine.

Additional Key Terms (from key-terms list, not covered above)

  • Hydrophobic ("water-fearing," repels water) vs. Hydrophilic ("water-loving," attracted to water).
  • Nonpolar molecules (no charge separation, typically hydrophobic, cross membranes easily) vs. Polar molecules (charge separation, hydrophilic, need help crossing).
  • Selectively permeable / Differentially permeable — describes the membrane allowing some substances through freely while restricting others.
  • Fluid mosaic model (flagged — marked with an asterisk on the study guide's own key-terms list; verify testability with your professor) — describes the membrane as a dynamic, ever-shifting ("fluid") mixture ("mosaic") of phospholipids and proteins.
  • Lysis (cell bursts from excess water uptake, hypotonic environment) vs. Plasmolysis (cell shrinks from water loss, hypertonic environment).
  • Proton Pump — an active-transport protein that moves H+ ions using energy, creating a proton gradient (motive force) used for other cellular work (e.g., powering flagella).
  • Gene / DNA / Chromosome — Gene = a DNA segment coding for a product; DNA = the double-stranded genetic material; Chromosome = the main DNA loop housing most genes, located in the nucleoid.
  • Svedberg units — your study guide notes state you do need to know these (used to measure ribosome size, e.g., 70S vs. 80S).

Microbe/Disease Spotlight - Bacillus (genus), Clostridium (genus), Clostridioides difficile — all endospore-forming; C. difficile specifically causes Diarrhea via the mechanism described in Q24.
